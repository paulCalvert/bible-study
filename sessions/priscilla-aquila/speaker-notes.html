Priscilla & Aquila: Marriage, Submission & Oneness

CMBC Men's Breakfast  |  September 2026
Core Thread: What does a Godly marriage look like? Priscilla and Aquila are our model — two becoming one through love and submission.
Draft runtime (Intro ~5 + Seg1 ~11.5-13.5 + Seg2 ~17-18 + Seg3 ~15.5 = ~49-52 min) does not yet include a Close — that section was deleted pending rebuild. Add its time back once built.
Intro  (~5 min, before prayer)
  • The Challenge (~2 min): Finding a good husband model in Scripture is harder than it sounds — more warnings than models
    • David — a great man, not a good husband. Multiple marriages he never dealt with (Michal)
    • Abraham — one of the better husbands, but plenty of what-not-to-do too (callback to the group's Abraham study)
    • Peter — Google kept suggesting him, but not much is actually known about this marriage
    • Boaz — great guy, but a courtship model, not a marriage model
  • The Turn (~1.5 min): The name that kept coming up was Aquila — kept getting passed over because the search was only for "the husband." Can't talk about Aquila without Priscilla
    • Trying to isolate the husband was trying to separate what God designed not to be separated (Matthew 19:6)
      "So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate." Matthew 19:6
  • The Payoff Fact (~45 sec): (Acts 18:2, 18, 26; Romans 16:3; 1 Corinthians 16:19; 2 Timothy 4:19) — named six times in the New Testament, and every single time, both names appear in the same verse. Never one without the other
  • Preview (~30 sec): Pray → Segment 1
Segment 1 — Meeting Priscilla & Aquila  (~11.5-13.5 min | Acts 15–18)
  • Paul's Rough Second Missionary Journey (~5.5-6 min):
    • Splits from Barnabas, takes Silas and Timothy, picks up Luke (Acts 15:36-41; 16:1-3, 10)
    • Blocked from Asia/Ephesus by the Spirit, then Bithynia too — two closed doors (Acts 16:6-8)
      • Like raising money for a Mexico mission trip and God saying "Nope — Cuba instead"
    • Flogged and imprisoned in Philippi — first time jailed for his faith. Leaves Luke behind to pastor (Acts 16:19-40)
    • Some success in Thessalonica, but opposition threatens the church — leaves at night for Berea (Acts 17:1-9)
    • Berea receives the gospel eagerly — then the Thessalonica mob marches 45 miles just to chase him out again (Acts 17:10-15)
      • Ever made someone mad enough to walk 2.5 days to run you out of town? Paul leaves Silas and Timothy behind, heads to Athens alone
    • Athens — preaches the gospel at the Areopagus to the city's most influential men, delivers one of the best sermons in the Bible, sneered out mid-argument (Acts 17:16-34)
      • Doesn't wait for Silas and Timothy like planned — leaves alone for Corinth
    • Corinth — maybe he never thought he'd actually set foot here. Not exotic/unfamiliar to him — biggest of the trip so far, sure, but he grew up in Tarsus and served out of Antioch, both bigger, more prestigious Roman cities. Corinth's different: he's heard the stories his whole life — no good Jewish man would be caught dead there, a modern-day Sodom (Acts 18:1)
      • "Corinthianize" (κορινθιάζω, koh-rin-thee-AH-zoh) was a real, attested Greek verb meaning "to practice fornication" — the reputation was real enough that being called "a Corinthian" without being from Corinth was an insult
      • Caveat, not necessarily for live delivery: modern scholars note this reputation technically traces to the OLDER Greek Corinth, leveled by Rome in 146 BC — the rebuilt Roman-era city Paul enters may not have deserved it by his day. Doesn't change how Paul grew up hearing about it.
    • Imagine what Paul's feeling by now: healing wounds, missing Luke, worried for Silas and Timothy, missing Barnabas, second-guessing Athens, wondering why he's even here. Back in Jerusalem, whatever the crowd size, Saul was near the top of it — trained under Gamaliel, carrying the chief priests' own authority. None of that status travels with him here
    • Heads for the Jewish quarter hoping to find a hospitable Jew to stay with, regroup, maybe go back to tentmaking (Acts 18:2-3)
      • Walks into Aquila's shop, asks to stay and earn his keep — before Paul can even bring up Jesus, Aquila asks HIM about the good news
  • Introducing Priscilla (~1-1.5 min): (Acts 18:2) Aquila and Paul hit it off fast — same faith, same trade — then: "You should meet my wife"
    • Acts 18:2 is the ONE place in Scripture their names sit apart (~10 words, Luke's narration) — every other of the six mentions, names are adjacent
  • What Do We Even Know About Them? (~4 min):
    • How are they even Christian? Aquila's from Pontus — named among the nations at Pentecost (Acts 2:9), or the gospel reached that region early regardless — Peter later writes to believers there (1 Peter 1:1)
    • More than average tentmakers — host multiple churches in their home, move constantly (Rome→Corinth→Ephesus→Rome→Ephesus). Most people never left their hometown — real means implied
    • Likely a mixed marriage — Aquila's explicitly called a Jew, Priscilla never is. Her name (Prisca) is an old Roman family name, and she's named first more often than not a real possibility, not settled fact — full sourcing in the take-home handout
    • Landing: whoever they were before — Jewish/Roman, laborer/well-to-do — none of it mattered once they were one. That's the whole point.
  • Arrival in Ephesus (~1-2 min): (Acts 18:18-21) Paul pastors Corinth about a year, then finally heads to Ephesus — brings P&A with him. Paul only stays briefly; they remain and help establish the Ephesian church
    • Maybe the Spirit blocked Paul from Ephesus earlier because He wanted Priscilla and Aquila there first — Paul just had to go pick them up.

Segment 2 — Priscilla, Aquila, and Apollos  (~17-18 min, deliberately over target | Acts 18:24-28, 1 Tim 2)
  • Part 1 — The Apollos Story
  • Transition (~15 sec): Paul's gone from Ephesus now — Priscilla and Aquila are the leaders left behind. Hottest segment of the day — give it the time and respect it deserves, don't rush or dodge it.
  • A New Hotshot Arrives (~30 sec): (Acts 18:24-25) Apollos — gifted, eloquent, knows his Scripture cold. One real problem: his gospel has gaps
  • Corrected Privately, Received Gladly (~1 min): (Acts 18:26-28; 1 Corinthians 3:6) P&A take him aside privately rather than correct him in public — the more honorable route culturally. He doesn't get defensive, takes it, and becomes an effective, respected teacher in Corinth
    • What a great team.
  • Was Priscilla the Lead Teacher? (~1 min): Name order in the text — Priscilla first (Acts 18:26) — is the same convention Luke uses elsewhere: "Barnabas and Saul" flips to "Paul and Barnabas" right when Paul steps into leadership (Acts 13). Applied here, it suggests Priscilla was at least an equal co-leader in this correction — maybe the primary one
    • If name order feels like it shouldn't matter, think about any project with multiple people on it — a work project, an academic paper, whatever. Even when the work is genuinely even across the board, everyone still wants their name listed first. Why? Because first name gets noticed — it implies the lead role, the credit, the prestige. Name order still matters today.
  • Is This a Problem? (~30 sec): Fair question — because it's a genuinely divisive issue in the church today, and Scripture doesn't let us dodge it. (1 Timothy 2) says something that sounds like it disagrees with what we just watched Priscilla do. Let's read it
  • Part 2 — Why Cultural Context Matters
  • Read the Passage (~1 min): 1 Timothy 2:9-15 aloud
    "Likewise also that women should adorn themselves in respectable apparel, with modesty and self-control, not with braided hair and gold or pearls or costly attire, but with what is proper for women who profess godliness — with good works. Let a woman learn quietly with all submissiveness. I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man; rather, she is to remain quiet. For Adam was formed first, then Eve; and Adam was not deceived, but the woman was deceived and became a transgressor. Yet she will be saved through childbearing — if they continue in faith and love and holiness, with self-control." 1 Timothy 2:9-15
  • But Wait, I Have Seen Women Teachers? (~30 sec): Women in church leadership already exist — e.g. Bishop Mariann Edgar Budde, who preached at the prayer service following the most recent presidential inauguration, and our own Pastor Ashley Bell here at Cedar Mill. So why, if Paul's letter seems so clear about forbidding it?
    • Worth sitting with: this isn't just a modern tension. 1 Timothy was written TO Ephesus — and Priscilla was a known fixture of that exact church community. The original hearers of "I do not permit a woman to teach" would have had Priscilla standing right there in the room, or fresh in memory. They'd have been thinking the same thing we are: "Wait — what about her?"
  • Cultural Context as a Tool (~45 sec): Not an excuse to explain away a hard passage — a tool to understand how it was written, and to whom, so we can read it the way its original audience would have
    • This approach has an actual name: hermeneutics — the principles used to interpret a text. Today we're covering the cultural-context side of it; Segment 3 picks the term back up with other passages.
  • Why Ephesus Mattered (~1 min): Guardian of the Roman imperial cult, a major banking center, seat of the province's governor — Asia was Rome's richest, most populous province, and Ephesus was its leading city.
    • Confirmed: 1 Corinthians (FROM Ephesus), 1 & 2 Timothy (TO Ephesus), Ephesians, Revelation (first of its seven churches)
    • Strong-argument, one reason each: John's Gospel/letters — early tradition places John living/writing in Ephesus post-Patmos; Titus — technically about Crete, but Crete shared a strikingly similar religious/cultural backdrop (more in Part 3); Philippians/Colossians/Philemon — minority theory Paul wrote these from an Ephesian imprisonment, not just his Roman one
  • Who Was Artemis? (~1 min): Ephesus's Artemis wasn't one god among many — ancient sources rank her the second-most-titled deity in the entire Greek pantheon, behind only Zeus. Pausanias himself wrote: "all cities worship Artemis of Ephesus, and individuals hold her in honor above all the gods" (Pausanias, Description of Greece 4.31.8)
    • Her temple: four times the size of the Parthenon, 127 sixty-foot columns — one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World
  • Part 3 — Reading 1 Timothy 2 Through Ephesus's Eyes
  • The Hair Instruction (~1 min): (1 Timothy 2:9) The hair instruction (πλέγμασιν, pleg-MAH-sin) shares a root with the hairstyle worn in Ephesus's Artemis processions (πεπλεγμένη, peh-pleg-MEH-nay) (Xenophon, Ephesiaca 1.2.6) — maybe a very specific, local look, not fashion in general
  • "Saved Through Childbearing" (~1 min): (1 Timothy 2:15) Artemis was, paradoxically, both a perpetual virgin and the goddess of childbirth — patron of safe delivery, or a painless death if delivery went wrong. That's the exact danger this verse is speaking into
    • Not a coincidence Paul reaches for "Savior" here, either — that title shows up concentrated almost exclusively in the Ephesus/Crete-connected letters (Titus, 1 Timothy) and is nearly absent everywhere else he writes
    • Ancient childbirth carried real mortality risk — ~1-2.5% per birth ("1 in 50"), compounding across pregnancies. Paul may be speaking to women genuinely afraid, both of that danger and of leaving Artemis (who held spiritual authority over exactly this) behind. His message: Jesus is your Savior, not her
      • Delivery note: ~1-2% sounds mild — but that's ~10x today's global maternal mortality rate (~0.2%, WHO 2023) and ~100x wealthy-nation rates (~0.003%). Say "10x more likely to die with each pregnancy" out loud, not just the raw percentage.
    • Artemis isn't the one who saves. Jesus is.
  • "Adam Was Formed First" (~1 min): (1 Timothy 2:13) Being firstborn was literally one of Artemis's own titles — a claim to preeminence and autonomy, since she was present at her twin Apollo's birth. Read against that backdrop, "Adam was formed first" may not be staking a claim to one-sided authority — it may be restoring interdependence against a culture that used being-first as a status claim
    • Possible second angle: early church fathers (Irenaeus, Tertullian) identify an incipient Gnostic heresy at Ephesus — the Kroegers' research argues some of it claimed Eve was actually made first, an "originator" with special status. If real, Paul may be correcting that specific local claim, not just making a general statement.
  • "I Do Not Permit a Woman to Teach" (~2.5 min): (1 Timothy 2:12) The hardest verse, and there's more than one thing likely going on. Moving quickly through these — full detail's in the handout
    • a. "Exercise authority" translates αὐθεντεῖν (ow-then-TEEN), a rare word that elsewhere can carry a sense as aggressive as "dominate" or "usurp" — this may be a specific, virulent kind of teaching Paul's shutting down, not teaching itself
    • b. The Artemis cult was fiercely competitive among its own priestesses — real civic and religious office, real status to fight for. Maybe that competitive instinct followed some women straight into the church
    • c. The Artemis cult was also famous for its wealthy female benefactors, and the early church leaned on generous women too — if a wealthy woman was used to getting her way through her giving in one system, she may have tried the same leverage in the other
    • d. Ephesus had a real, documented history with magic — Paul's own ministry there ends with a mass book-burning of magic scrolls (Acts 19:18-19). Some scholars read "busybodies" later in this same letter (1 Timothy 5:13) as a term for practicing magic, not gossip — maybe the issue was some women trying to bring old practices into the church
    • e. Zoom out, and it doesn't look like a women-only problem. Just before this (1 Timothy 2:8), Paul tells the men to pray "without anger or quarreling" — sounds like the whole Ephesian congregation was unruly, not just the women in it
    • Landing: this reads less like a universal, timeless rule and more like Paul putting out a specific fire in one specific church.
  • Part 4 — Back to Priscilla & Aquila
  • Does That Fully Resolve It? (~1 min): That changes how we read the passage in Timothy a bit, doesn't it? Maybe not completely — scholars still debate Ephesus-specific vs. universal. If you're still not sure how to land this passage, go back to the story we just watched: how did the men handle it?
  • Aquila Welcomed It (~1 min): He didn't cut Priscilla out of the correction because it broke some assumed "gender norm" — he welcomed the chance to work alongside his wife
  • Apollos Welcomed It (~1 min): (Acts 18:26) A gifted young religious leader welcoming correction at all is rare. Welcoming it from a woman, without balking, is rarer still
  • Emulate the Men (~1 min):
    • Like Aquila — welcome your wife in her strengths, and give her real opportunity to lead alongside you.
    • Like Apollos — stay humble enough to learn from whomever God speaks to you through, including a woman teacher.

Segment 3 — Marriage, Submission & Oneness  (~15.5 min | Eph 5, Gen 2:24)
  • Transition (~30 sec): Covered who they are, their journey, Priscilla correcting Apollos. What about their marriage?
  • Ephesians 5 and the Tension (~2 min): Read Ephesians 5:22-33 aloud
    "Wives, submit to your own husbands, as to the Lord. For the husband is the head of the wife even as Christ is the head of the church, his body, and is himself its Savior. Now as the church submits to Christ, so also wives should submit in everything to their husbands. Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing of water with the word, so that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle or any such thing, that she might be holy and without blemish. In the same way husbands should love their wives as their own bodies. He who loves his wife loves himself. For no one ever hated his own flesh, but nourishes and cherishes it, just as Christ does the church, because we are members of his body. 'Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh.' This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church. However, let each one of you love his wife as himself, and let the wife see that she respects her husband." Ephesians 5:22-33
    • Just notice — Paul calls Jesus "Savior" in this passage too. Same as what we talked about earlier.
    • This passage shaped complementarian marriage teaching ever since — spawned books like Love & Respect, Wild at Heart. Genuinely helped a lot of marriages
    • Sits in real tension with "neither male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28)
    • If God makes no distinction for gender, why is Paul making one here?
  • Mutual Submission + Timeless vs. Cultural Truths (~1.5 min): (Ephesians 5:21) "Submitting to one another" has no gender restriction — only verse 22 narrows to wives
    "...submitting to one another out of reverence for Christ. Wives, submit to your own husbands, as to the Lord." Ephesians 5:21-22
    • This mutual-submission command transcends time and culture, the same way this does — true everywhere, always (Romans 8:38-39)
      "For I am sure that neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers, nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord." Romans 8:38-39
    • Some of what follows is tied to the culture it was written into, and needs to be read differently
    • The same pattern shows up for love, not just submission: (Ephesians 5:1-2) "walk in love, as Christ loved us" is a general command to everyone — v.25's "husbands, love your wives" narrows it to one spouse, the same way 5:21's "submit to one another" narrows to wives in v.22. Both universal commands get a gender-specific application right after.
  • How Do We Navigate These Cultural Truths? (~1 min): [Slide — image from Webb's Slaves, Women & Homosexuals]
    • Beyond cultural context alone, the Bible has a redemptive arc — instructions progressive for their time can look regressive as culture surpasses them
  • Levirate Marriage (~2 min): (Deuteronomy 25:5-10) A widow marries her husband's brother — sounds backward today
    "If brothers dwell together, and one of them dies and has no son, the wife of the dead man shall not be married outside the family to a stranger. Her husband's brother shall go in to her and take her as his wife and perform the duty of a husband's brother to her. And the first son whom she bears shall succeed to the name of his dead brother, that his name may not be blotted out of Israel. And if the man does not wish to take his brother's wife, then his brother's wife shall go up to the gate to the elders and say, 'My husband's brother refuses to perpetuate his brother's name in Israel; he will not perform the duty of a husband's brother to me.' Then the elders of his city shall call him and speak to him, and if he persists, saying, 'I do not wish to take her,' then his brother's wife shall go up to him in the presence of the elders and pull his sandal off his foot and spit in his face. And she shall answer and say, 'So shall it be done to the man who does not build up his brother's house.' And the name of his house shall be called in Israel, 'The house of him who had his sandal pulled off.'" Deuteronomy 25:5-10
    • Genuinely protective at the time: women couldn't reliably earn income, were vulnerable without a husband, children carried real weight
    • Not redemptive today — the vulnerabilities it addressed have largely changed
  • Why Paul Wrote What He Wrote (~1.5 min): (1 Corinthians 9:22) Could've pushed a more countercultural marital ethic — chose reconciling people to God over social revolution
    "I have become all things to all people, that I might by all means save some." 1 Corinthians 9:22
    • Same instinct we already saw in Segment 2 — working within Ephesus's existing culture rather than picking every possible fight at once.
  • Reading Ephesians 5 Now (~2 min):
    • Mutual submission applies to everyone — in marriage specifically, wives submit and husbands love. Likely about directing each spouse's existing social strength toward the gospel; a matriarchal culture might have gotten the direction flipped, principle unchanged
    • Notice what Paul does NOT say: he never tells husbands to "rule" or "lead" — the reciprocal of "submit." He tells them to love, and adds a steep standard of accountability on top of it. He assumes the culture's existing status quo for women, but stretches the men's growth — pushing the boundary, without snapping it
    • Today women have far more representation and freedom than ancient Ephesus — we're not bound to Paul's city-and-time-specific advice
    • But love and submission themselves are NOT culturally bound — commanded to the whole church, not a one-time cultural application.
  • Two Become One (~2 min): (Genesis 2:24) Marriage's original purpose — love and submission both required to produce that unity
    • Opposite of submission is defiance. Opposite of love is indifference
    • "Husbands love, wives submit" is the headline — but husbands need to learn submission too, wives need to learn to love actively too
    • Also an edification tool — someone that close helps us walk with God better
    • Slide reinforces this directly: Submission and Love are both commanded to everyone (Ephesians 5:1-2, 5:21) — Defiance and Apathy are what breaks unity instead.
    • Personal: I have no problem with submission. If I'm having a hard day in our marriage with Lauren, I tend to pull away and withhold love instead — I become apathetic, and that's what breaks our unity
  • What Do You Need to Work On? (~1 min): Two scenarios, read straight from the slide: Husband says "Thank God I don't need to submit to my wife, I couldn't bear that" — Paul(s)*: "What a wonderful opportunity to learn and grow in submission!" Wife says "I'm okay submitting, but at least I don't have to feel anything toward him" — Paul(s)*: "God wants us to love and desire oneness with one another. Keep up the good fight, sister."
    • *Both the Apostle Paul and Paul Calvert, weighing in — call out the joke live.
  • Back to Priscilla & Aquila (~1 min): Segment 2 already made the case that Priscilla was at least an equal co-leader in that one correction — but it's not limited to that one episode. Across their whole appearance in Scripture, Priscilla reads as at least a co-leader, quite possibly the primary one, in the couple's broader work of church planting and evangelism
    • Landing: whichever of them held more visible status at a given moment, they appear as essentially one throughout the text — partners in the mission, practicing mutual love and submission together. Back to where we started: no longer two, but one.
  • Application For Us (~1 min): May look different depending on the relationship or cultural context — but Paul's call to submit and love applies to everyone. Work with your spouse to figure out what unity actually requires right now
