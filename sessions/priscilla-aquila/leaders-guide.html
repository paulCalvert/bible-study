Leader's Guide

Priscilla & Aquila: Marriage, Submission & Oneness

Event: CMBC Men's Breakfast  |  Date: September 2026  |  Materials by: Paul Calvert
Intro — Before Prayer  (~5 min)
1. The Challenge (~2 min)
  • Finding a good husband model in Scripture is harder than it sounds. There are more warnings than models.
    • David — a great man, not a good husband. Multiple marriages he never fully dealt with, which kept him from repairing broken ones. Michal is the clearest example.
    • Abraham — one of the better husbands, but lots of examples of what not to do at the same time. (Callback to the group's prior Abraham study.)
    • Peter — Google kept recommending this, but not a lot is known about this marriage.
    • Boaz — great guy, but a courtship model, not a marriage model. Different lesson.
2. The Turn (~1.5 min)
  • The name that kept coming up was Aquila — and kept getting passed over, because the search was only for "the husband." You cannot talk about Aquila without Priscilla.
  • I realized that I was trying to isolate it to just the husband, but that is not what God designed. I was trying to separate what should not be separated. (Matthew 19:6 — "So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate.")
3. The Payoff Fact (~45 sec)
Acts 18:2, 18:18, 18:26; Romans 16:3; 1 Corinthians 16:19; 2 Timothy 4:19
  • Priquila (ship name) are named six times in the New Testament. Every single time, both names appear in the same verse. Not once do we get one without the other, anywhere in Scripture. They are a model of what it means of two becoming one.
4. Pray (~30 sec)
  • Pray, then move into Segment 1.
Segment 1 — Meeting Priscilla & Aquila  (~11.5-13.5 min | Acts 15-18)
"To learn about Priquila we need to start with Paul and his second missionary journey. It was actually pretty rough. Let's go through what his journey was like through Acts, and I will fill in some gaps with what he MAY have been feeling, to help build the narrative a bit. I'd encourage you to read through these passages yourself afterward and try to imagine what Paul must have been feeling throughout."
1. Paul's Rough Second Missionary Journey (~5.5-6 min)
  • Splits from Barnabas — sharp disagreement — takes Silas and Timothy instead. He eventually picks up Luke as well — his physician. (Acts 15:36-41; 16:1-3; 16:10)
  • Blocked from Asia/Ephesus by the Holy Spirit, then blocked from Bithynia too — two closed doors. (Acts 16:6-8)
    • Imagine raising money and planning to go to Mexico on a mission trip. But on the way God says, "Nope. You are going to Cuba instead." (Cuba's official/spoken language is Spanish too, in case you want to riff live: "I practiced Spanish for Mexico. What do they speak in Cuba??? Spanish, I hope???")
  • Paul is led to Philippi, where he is flogged and imprisoned — first time he is jailed for his faith. Then leaves Luke behind to pastor the young church. (Acts 16:19-40)
  • Paul has some success in Thessalonica (church of Thessalonians), but strong opposition threaten other members of the church. Paul and crew leave at night for Berea. (Acts 17:1-9)
  • In Berea, the people eagerly receive the gospel and things are going great! BUT THEN PEOPLE STILL PISSED AT HIM FROM THESSALONICA MARCH 45 MILES JUST TO CHASE PAUL OUT AGAIN. (Acts 17:10-15)
    • Have you ever pissed anyone off so bad that they walked 2 1/2 days to chase you out of town? Because Paul did. Paul leaves for Athens to help relieve pressure on the church, and leaves Silas and Timothy behind to shepherd the church.
  • Paul is alone in Athens now, an impressive city filled with idolatry and philosophy. He tries to wait for Silas and Timothy, but he cannot help himself and begins to spread the Gospel. Then he is invited to the Areopagus to share the Gospel to all of the most influential men of the city! (Acts 17:16-34)
    • Paul delivers one of the best sermons in the Bible, adapted to the culture and people of a secular/philosophical background. Before he can finish though, they sneer and kick him out. A handful follow him, but I imagine he is discouraged even more.
    • Paul doesn't wait for Silas and Timothy like he planned, he just leaves for Corinth alone.
  • Maybe Paul never thought he'd ever actually set foot here. This isn't some big, exotic city he's unfamiliar with — biggest of the trip so far, sure, but he grew up in Tarsus and more recently served out of Antioch: big, impressive, prestigious Roman cities in their own right. Corinth is different. He's heard the stories about Corinth his whole life — no good Jewish man would be caught dead in Corinth, a modern-day Sodom. (Acts 18:1)
    • It wasn't exaggeration for effect, either — the Greeks had literally coined a verb for it: "Corinthianize" (κορινθιάζω, pronounced koh-rin-thee-AH-zoh), a real, attested word meaning "to practice fornication." Corinth's reputation was so notorious that if you were called a "Corinthian" and weren't actually from Corinth, you'd take it as an insult.
  • What do we think Paul is feeling at this time?
    • His flogging wounds may still be healing and sore. He might be missing his doctor Luke.
    • Thinking of Luke, he remembers Silas and Timothy. He worries for their safety — are the Thessalonians still going after them?
    • He even begins to miss Barnabas. "I could really use some encouragement. Why was their fight such a big deal again? If only my pride did not get in the way back then."
    • Flinching at wounded pride, he remembers the jeers and heckles from the Areopagus. Did he mess up? Should he have not made it so academic, and brought Jesus up sooner?
    • Looks around Corinth — "What am I even doing here? I am tired, hungry, hurting, and so lonely. Do they even want to know about God? I did not even want to be here. I wanted to be in Ephesus!"
  • "Maybe... I should just be making tents. Maybe I should just get back to that. I am low on money anyways, and tents do not imprison, beat, or mock me." Paul navigates to the labour district, and finds the Jewish quarter. If he could just find a hospitable Jew, he can stay a few nights and regroup a bit. Try and salvage this mission trip. (Acts 18:2-3)
  • Paul walks into a shop, big enough for him to stay the night without getting in the way of the others. He asks if he can just stay for a bit, and earn his keep. The man, named Aquila, says, "No worries, brother." Internal push from the Holy Spirit to speak up, resisted twice. Right in the middle of Paul's hesitation, Aquila says: "Hey — have you heard the good news about Jesus?" (Acts 18:2-3)
  • Now, this journey may not have played out EXACTLY like just shared. Again, I encourage you to re-read Paul's second missionary trip in Acts on your own time. But really, how do you think Paul was feeling to see Aquila in this moment? I am confident he was ECSTATIC.
2. Introducing Priscilla (~1-1.5 min)
Acts 18:2
  • Paul and Aquila hit it off fast — same faith, same trade. Then Aquila says, "You should meet my wife."
  • Personal illustration: contrast with how naturally Aquila treated Priscilla as inseparable from himself, versus a more typical "I made a friend, eventually he met my wife" pattern.
  • Acts 18:2 is the only point in Scripture where Priscilla and Aquila's names are separated by any real distance — about 10 words, due to Luke's narration. Every other of the six mentions places their names directly adjacent. Confirmed as the widest gap between them anywhere in Scripture.
3. What Do We Even Know About Priscilla and Aquila? (Turns Out, a Lot!) (~4 min)
  • How are they even Christian? Aquila is from Pontus — named among the nations present at Pentecost (Acts 2:9). Even if not Pentecost directly, the gospel reached that region early — Peter later writes an entire letter addressed to believers there (1 Peter 1:1), meaning mission work had already happened.
  • More than the average tentmaker. Paul's letters to Ephesus and Corinth show them hosting churches — plural — in their own home, unusual for laboring-class people. They also moved constantly: Rome to Corinth to Ephesus to Rome to Ephesus. Most people in the ancient world never left their hometown. Real financial means implied.
  • Likely a mixed marriage. Aquila is explicitly called a Jew in the text; Priscilla never is. Her name, Prisca, is a genuine old Roman family name (gens Prisca). She's named first more often than not — unusual for the culture. Together: a real possibility Priscilla came from a resourced, notable Roman background, married to a working Jewish tentmaker. Not settled fact — full sourcing in the take-home research handout.
  • Whoever they were before they met — Jewish and Roman, laborer and possibly more well-to-do — none of it mattered once they were one. That's the whole point.
4. Arrival in Ephesus (~1-2 min)
Acts 18:18-21
  • Paul pastors the Corinthian church for about a year, and then goes to Ephesus. FINALLY! And he brings Priscilla and Aquila with him. While Paul only stays briefly in Ephesus actually, Priquila remains in Ephesus and helps establish the Ephesian church.
  • The Holy Spirit kept Paul from going to Ephesus at the beginning of his 2nd mission trip, possibly for a myriad of reasons that we are not going into right now. I personally think that another plausible reason is that God wanted Priscilla and Aquila there in Ephesus to help establish the church. The problem was not that Paul wanted to go to Ephesus, he just had to pick them up first.

Segment 2 — Priscilla, Aquila, and Apollos  (~17-18 min | Acts 18:24-28, 1 Timothy 2)
"Okay, back in Ephesus. Paul is now gone, but Priscilla and Aquila remain as leaders in the church there. Now I will warn you, this is the HOTTEST of our three segments today. I want to take the time instead of rushing things or being dismissive, to give this topic the respect and time it deserves."
Part 1 — The Apollos Story
1. A New Hotshot Arrives (~30 sec)
Acts 18:24-25
  • Apollos — gifted, eloquent, knows his Scripture cold. One real problem: his gospel has gaps.
2. Corrected Privately, Received Gladly (~1 min)
Acts 18:26-28; 1 Corinthians 3:6
  • Priscilla and Aquila take him aside privately rather than correct him in public — the more honorable route culturally. He doesn't get defensive. He takes it, and goes on to become an effective, respected teacher in Corinth.
  • What a great team.
3. Was Priscilla the Lead Teacher? (~1 min)
Acts 18:26
  • Name order in the text — Priscilla first — is the same convention Luke uses elsewhere: "Barnabas and Saul" flips to "Paul and Barnabas" right when Paul steps into leadership (Acts 13). Applied here, it suggests Priscilla was at least an equal co-leader in this correction — maybe the primary one.
  • If name order feels like it shouldn't matter, think about any project with multiple people on it — a work project, an academic paper, whatever. Even when the work is genuinely even across the board, everyone still wants their name listed first. Why? Because first name gets noticed — it implies the lead role, the credit, the prestige. Name order still matters today.
4. Is This a Problem? (~30 sec)
  • Fair question — because it's a genuinely divisive issue in the church today, and Scripture doesn't let us dodge it. 1 Timothy 2 says something that sounds like it disagrees with what we just watched Priscilla do. Let's read it.
Part 2 — Why Cultural Context Matters
5. Read the Passage (~1 min)
1 Timothy 2:9-15
  • Read 1 Timothy 2:9-15 aloud — full text on the slide.
6. But Wait, I Have Seen Women Teachers? (~30 sec)
  • You may have seen women in church leadership before — pictured here, for example, is Bishop Mariann Edgar Budde, the Episcopal minister who preached at the prayer service following the most recent presidential inauguration. Even here at Cedar Mill, we have our own Pastor Ashley Bell. So why are women in church leadership if Paul's letter seems so clear about forbidding it?
7. Cultural Context as a Tool (~45 sec)
  • Not an excuse to explain away a hard passage — a tool to understand how it was written, and to whom, so we can read it the way its original audience would have.
  • This approach has an actual name: hermeneutics — the principles used to interpret a text. Today we're covering the cultural-context side of it; Segment 3 picks the term back up with other passages.
8. Why Ephesus Mattered (~1 min)
  • Guardian of the Roman imperial cult, a major banking center, seat of the province's governor — Asia was Rome's richest, most populous province, and Ephesus was its leading city.
  • Confirmed New Testament books written from or to Ephesus: 1 Corinthians (FROM Ephesus), 1 & 2 Timothy (TO Ephesus), Ephesians, and Revelation (Ephesus is literally the first of its seven churches).
    • Strong-argument books, one reason each: John's Gospel and letters — early tradition places John living and writing in Ephesus during and after his exile on Patmos
    • Titus — technically about Crete, not Ephesus, but Crete shared a strikingly similar religious and cultural backdrop, particularly the pieces we're about to get into (more in Part 3)
    • Philippians, Colossians, and Philemon — a real minority theory holds Paul wrote these from an Ephesian imprisonment, not only his Roman one
9. Who Was Artemis? (~1 min)
  • Ephesus's Artemis wasn't one god among many — ancient sources rank her the second-most-titled deity in the entire Greek pantheon, behind only Zeus. Pausanias himself wrote: "all cities worship Artemis of Ephesus, and individuals hold her in honor above all the gods" (Pausanias, Description of Greece 4.31.8).
  • Her temple: four times the size of the Parthenon, 127 sixty-foot columns — one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.
Part 3 — Reading 1 Timothy 2 Through Ephesus's Eyes
10. The Hair Instruction (~1 min)
1 Timothy 2:9
  • The hair instruction (πλέγμασιν, pronounced pleg-MAH-sin) shares a root with the hairstyle worn in Ephesus's Artemis processions (πεπλεγμένη, pronounced peh-pleg-MEH-nay), confirmed against Xenophon's Ephesiaca 1.2.6 — maybe a very specific, local look, not fashion in general.
11. "Saved Through Childbearing" (~1 min)
1 Timothy 2:15
  • Artemis was, paradoxically, both a perpetual virgin and the goddess of childbirth — patron of safe delivery, or a painless death if delivery went wrong. That's the exact danger this verse is speaking into. One of her own titles, in fact, was "Savior."
  • Not a coincidence Paul reaches for "Savior" here, either — that title shows up concentrated almost exclusively in the Ephesus/Crete-connected letters (Titus, 1 Timothy) and is nearly absent everywhere else he writes.
  • Ancient childbirth carried real, well-documented mortality risk — estimates for the Roman world generally run somewhere around 1-2.5% per birth (some scholars cite "1 in 50"), which compounds fast across multiple pregnancies. Paul may be speaking directly to women genuinely afraid — both of that real physical danger, and of leaving Artemis (who held real spiritual authority over exactly this danger) behind by becoming Christian. His message: Jesus is your Savior and your hope, not her.
  • You hear "1-2%" and might think that's not so bad — but that's roughly 10x higher than the global maternal mortality rate today (~0.2%, WHO 2023), and closer to 100x higher than wealthy nations specifically (Australia/New Zealand sit at ~0.003%). Worth stating the multiple out loud, not just the raw percentage — "10 times more likely to die with each pregnancy" lands harder than "1-2%" does. And that mortality number is just death — it says nothing about whether the child survives, or whether the mother comes through with a horrifically painful or permanently crippling delivery. The real danger was even broader than the raw stat suggests.
  • Artemis isn't the one who saves. Jesus is.
12. "Adam Was Formed First" (~1 min)
1 Timothy 2:13
  • Being firstborn was literally one of Artemis's own titles — a claim to preeminence and autonomy, since she was present at her twin Apollo's birth. Read against that backdrop, "Adam was formed first" may not be staking a claim to one-sided authority — it may be restoring interdependence against a culture that used being-first as a status claim.
  • Possible second angle: early church fathers (Irenaeus, Tertullian) identify an incipient Gnostic heresy at Ephesus — the Kroegers' research argues some of it claimed Eve was actually made first, an "originator" with special status. If real, Paul may be correcting that specific local claim, not just making a general statement.
13. "I Do Not Permit a Woman to Teach" (~2.5 min)
1 Timothy 2:12
  • The hardest verse, and there's more than one thing likely going on. Move quickly through these — full detail's in the take-home handout.
  • a. "Exercise authority" translates αὐθεντεῖν (pronounced ow-then-TEEN), a rare word that elsewhere can carry a sense as aggressive as "dominate" or "usurp" — this may be a specific, virulent kind of teaching Paul's shutting down, not teaching itself.
  • b. The Artemis cult was fiercely competitive among its own priestesses — real civic and religious office, real status to fight for. Maybe that competitive instinct followed some women straight into the church.
  • c. The Artemis cult was also famous for its wealthy female benefactors, and the early church leaned on generous women too — if a wealthy woman was used to getting her way through her giving in one system, she may have tried the same leverage in the other.
  • d. Ephesus had a real, documented history with magic — Paul's own ministry there ends with a mass book-burning of magic scrolls (Acts 19:18-19). Some scholars read "busybodies" later in this same letter (1 Timothy 5:13) as a term for practicing magic, not gossip — maybe the issue was some women trying to bring old practices into the church.
  • e. Zoom out, and it doesn't look like a women-only problem. Just before this (1 Timothy 2:8), Paul tells the men to pray "without anger or quarreling" — sounds like the whole Ephesian congregation was unruly, not just the women in it.
  • This reads less like a universal, timeless rule and more like Paul putting out a specific fire in one specific church.
Part 4 — Back to Priscilla & Aquila
14. Does That Fully Resolve It? (~1 min)
1 Timothy 2:9-15
  • That changes how we read the passage in Timothy a bit, doesn't it?
  • Maybe not completely — scholars still debate Ephesus-specific vs. universal. If you're still not sure how to land this passage, go back to the story we just watched: how did the men handle it?
15. Aquila Welcomed It (~1 min)
  • He didn't cut Priscilla out of the correction because it broke some assumed "gender norm" — he welcomed the chance to work alongside his wife.
16. Apollos Welcomed It (~1 min)
Acts 18:26
  • A gifted young religious leader welcoming correction at all is rare. Welcoming it from a woman, without balking, is rarer still.
17. Emulate the Men (~1 min)
  • Like Aquila — welcome your wife in her strengths, and give her real opportunity to lead alongside you.
  • Like Apollos — stay humble enough to learn from whomever God speaks to you through, including a woman teacher.

Segment 3 — Marriage, Submission & Oneness  (~15.5 min | Eph 5, Gen 2:24)
"We've covered who Priscilla and Aquila are, their journey, and how Priscilla correcting Apollos sits alongside Paul's instruction on women and teaching. But what about their marriage?"
1. Ephesians 5 and the Tension (~2 min)
Ephesians 5:22-33; Galatians 3:28
  • Read Ephesians 5:22-33 aloud — full text on the slide.
  • Just notice — Paul calls Jesus "Savior" in this passage too. Same as what we talked about earlier.
  • This passage has shaped complementarian marriage teaching ever since — it's spawned books like Love & Respect, Wild at Heart. It has genuinely helped a lot of marriages.
  • But it sits in real tension with Galatians 3:28 — "There is neither Jew nor Greek... nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus."
  • If God then makes no distinction for gender, then why is Paul making the distinction in this passage?
2. Mutual Submission + Timeless vs. Cultural Truths (~1.5 min)
Ephesians 5:21; Romans 8:38-39
  • Read Ephesians 5:21 alongside 5:22 — full text on the slide. No gender restriction on the first command — it's only 5:22 that narrows to wives.
  • This mutual-submission command is one that transcends time and culture — the same way Romans 8:38-39 does (also on the slide). True everywhere, always.
  • But some of the commands that follow are tied to the culture they were written into, and need to be evaluated differently.
  • The same pattern shows up for love, not just submission: Ephesians 5:1-2 — "walk in love, as Christ loved us" — is a general command to everyone. Verse 25's "husbands, love your wives" narrows it to one spouse, the same way 5:21's "submit to one another" narrows to wives in v.22. Both universal commands get a gender-specific application right after.
3. How Do We Navigate These Cultural Truths? (~1 min)
  • Beyond cultural context alone (like what we saw in 1 Timothy and Ephesus), the Bible has a redemptive arc.
  • Cultural instructions tied to that redemption narrative for repressed groups were progressive relative to their original culture — but become more regressive over time as the culture surpasses them.
4. Levirate Marriage (~2 min)
  • This seems backwards today. Are the men just trading women around like property? Also, why is the brother punished for NOT wanting to marry his dead brother's wife? It seems reasonable to me, but then he's publicly shamed? (Full passage on the slide.)
  • At the time, it was genuinely protective: women couldn't reliably earn income, were vulnerable without a husband's protection, and children carried real social and spiritual weight. This law protected women who'd otherwise be pushed to the margins.
  • Not redemptive today — the vulnerabilities it addressed have largely changed in today's date and culture.
5. Why Paul Wrote What He Wrote (~1.5 min)
1 Corinthians 9:22
  • Paul could have pushed the church toward a more countercultural marital ethic. He chose the priority of reconciling people to God over social revolution — full quote on the slide.
  • Same instinct we already saw in Segment 2 — working within Ephesus's existing culture rather than picking every possible fight at once.
6. Reading Ephesians 5 Now (~2 min)
  • Paraphrase the Ephesian passages one more time.
  • The mutual-submission command applies to everyone — but in marriage specifically, Paul directs wives to submit and husbands to love. Likely about directing each spouse's existing social strength toward the gospel — if Paul had written to a matriarchal culture, the specific direction might have flipped while the underlying principle stayed the same.
  • Notice what Paul does NOT say: he never tells husbands to "rule" or "lead" — the reciprocal of "submit." He tells them to love, and adds a steep standard of accountability on top of it. He assumes the culture's existing status quo for women, but stretches the men's growth — pushing the boundary, without snapping it.
  • Today, outside the church, women have far more representation and freedom than in ancient Ephesus. We're not bound to Paul's culturally-specific advice to that city and time — we look toward where God is drawing the kingdom next.
  • But love and submission themselves are NOT culturally bound. Paul commanded them to the whole church, not as a one-time cultural application.
7. Two Become One (~2 min)
Genesis 2:24
  • "Love and submission, okay. Why are they so important?"
  • Marriage's original purpose in Scripture: two becoming one (Genesis 2:24).
  • Love and submission are both necessary to produce that unity. The opposite of submission is defiance. The opposite of love is indifference.
  • "Husbands love your wives, wives submit to your husbands" is the headline — but husbands also need to learn submission, wives also need to learn to love actively.
  • This is also an edification tool — having someone that close helps us walk with God better.
  • Slide reinforces this directly: Submission and Love are both commanded to everyone (Ephesians 5:1-2, 5:21) — Defiance and Apathy are what breaks unity instead.
  • For me personally, I have no problem with submission. Rather, if I'm having a hard day in our marriage with Lauren, I tend to pull away and withhold love from her. I become apathetic, which breaks the unity of our marriage.
8. What Do You Need to Work On? (~1 min)
  • Two scenarios, read straight from the slide: Husband says "Thank God I don't need to submit to my wife, I couldn't bear that" — Paul(s)*: "What a wonderful opportunity to learn and grow in submission!" Wife says "I'm okay submitting, but at least I don't have to feel anything toward him" — Paul(s)*: "God wants us to love and desire oneness with one another. Keep up the good fight, sister."
  • *Both the Apostle Paul and Paul Calvert, weighing in — call out the joke live.
9. Back to Priscilla & Aquila (~1 min)
  • Segment 2 already made the case that Priscilla was at least an equal co-leader in that one correction of Apollos — but it's not limited to that one episode.
  • Across their whole appearance in Scripture, Priscilla reads as at least a co-leader, quite possibly the primary one, in the couple's broader work of church planting and evangelism.
  • Whichever of them held more visible status at a given moment, they appear as essentially one throughout the text — partners in the mission, practicing mutual love and submission together. Back to where we started: no longer two, but one.
10. Application For Us (~1 min)
  • This may look different depending on the relationship, the situation, or the cultural context — but Paul's call to submit and love applies to everyone.
  • Work with your spouse to figure out what unity actually requires right now, and let that unity fuel the gospel's advance through your marriage.
