Genesis 2:18-24

2. Priscilla & Aquila (Priquila) — More Than a Man: A Unified Team (~30-45 sec)
  • The name that kept coming up was Aquila — and kept getting passed over, because the search was only for "the husband." You cannot talk about Aquila without Priscilla.

1. Paul's Rough Second Missionary Journey (~6.5-7 min — timed from an actual practice run)
  • Splits from Barnabas — sharp disagreement — takes Silas and Timothy instead. He eventually picks up Luke as well — his physician. (Acts 15:36-41; 16:1-3; 16:10)
  • Blocked from Asia/Ephesus by the Holy Spirit — redirected straight to Greece instead. (Acts 16:6-8)
  • Paul is led to Philippi, where he is flogged and imprisoned — first time he is jailed for his faith. Then leaves Luke behind to pastor the young church. (Acts 16:19-40)
  • Paul has some success in Thessalonica (church of Thessalonians), but strong opposition threatens other members of the church. Paul and crew leave, possibly to protect them, and walk 45 miles to Berea. (Acts 17:1-9)
  • In Berea, the people eagerly receive the gospel and things are going great! BUT THEN PEOPLE STILL PISSED AT HIM FROM THESSALONICA MARCH 45 MILES JUST TO CHASE PAUL OUT AGAIN. Paul leaves Silas and Timothy behind — maybe to keep teaching the Bereans — and heads to Athens alone, planning to catch up later. (Acts 17:10-15)
  • Paul is alone in Athens now, an impressive city filled with idolatry and philosophy. He tries to wait for Silas and Timothy, but he cannot help himself and begins to spread the Gospel. The city's who's-who hears him and invites him to the Areopagus — "come to the cool kids table." (Acts 17:16-34)
    • Paul delivers one of the best sermons in the Bible, adapted to the culture and people of a secular/philosophical background. Right as he's building to the climax — right before he can get to Jesus — they cut him off: "You're boring. Don't call us, we'll call you." A handful follow him, but I imagine he is discouraged even more.
    • Paul doesn't wait for Silas and Timothy like he planned, he just leaves for Corinth alone.
  • He's heard the stories about Corinth his whole life — no good Jewish man would be caught dead there, a modern-day Sodom. There's slang from that era too: to "act like a Corinthian" means to sleep around. Maybe his own parents even warned him about the place growing up. (Acts 18:1)
  • What do we think Paul is feeling at this time?
    • His flogging wounds may still be healing and sore. He might be missing his doctor Luke.
    • Thinking of Luke, he remembers Silas and Timothy. He worries for their safety — are the Thessalonians still going after them?
    • He even begins to miss Barnabas. "I could really use some encouragement. Why was their fight such a big deal again? If only my pride did not get in the way back then."
    • Flinching at wounded pride, he remembers the jeers and heckles from the Areopagus. "Did I just blow the chance of a lifetime?"
    • Looks around Corinth — "I wanted to go to Athens! I wanted to go to Ephesus! I didn't want to be here. Maybe I'm just better off making tents."
  • But within Corinth, he finds a ray of light: he meets Aquila and Priscilla — Jewish tentmakers who are already Christians. We don't fully know exactly how he met them — maybe by accident, maybe he sought them out after hearing about them from other believers — but he's able to stay with them, and it marks a real turning point. He's able to settle down and spend a full year in Corinth without being kicked out, building up the church there. (Acts 18:1-3)
  • Silas and Timothy catch up too. This is the turning point — Priscilla and Aquila bring life and encouragement to Paul right when he's in a dark place.
2. What Do We Even Know About Priscilla and Aquila? (Turns Out, a Lot!) (~3.5 min)
  • How are they even Christian? Aquila is from Pontus — named among the nations present at Pentecost (Acts 2:9). It's possible he became a Christian right there — meaning he might have been a Christian longer than Paul was. Even if not Pentecost directly, the gospel reached that region early — Peter later writes an entire letter addressed to believers there (1 Peter 1:1), meaning mission work had already happened.
  • More than the average tentmaker. Paul's letters to Ephesus and Corinth show them hosting churches — plural — in their own home, unusual for laboring-class people. They also moved constantly: Rome to Corinth to Ephesus to Rome to Ephesus. Most people in the ancient world never left their hometown. Real financial means implied.
  • Likely a mixed marriage. Aquila is explicitly called a Jew in the text; Priscilla never is. Her name, Prisca, is a genuine old Roman family name (gens Prisca). (Watch out live — easy to slip and say "Prissa.") She's named first more often than not — unusual for the culture. Together: a real possibility Priscilla came from a resourced, notable Roman background, married to a working Jewish tentmaker. Not settled fact — full sourcing in the take-home research handout.
  • Whoever they were before they met — Jewish and Roman, laborer and possibly more well-to-do — none of it mattered once they were one. That's the whole point.
3. Arrival in Ephesus (~45 sec-1 min)
Acts 18:18-21
  • Paul pastors the Corinthian church for about a year, and then goes to Ephesus. FINALLY! And he brings Priscilla and Aquila with him. Paul only stays briefly — he'll come back later for his third missionary trip — but when he leaves, Priscilla and Aquila remain to help establish the Ephesian church.
  • The Holy Spirit kept Paul from going to Ephesus at the beginning of his 2nd mission trip, possibly for a myriad of reasons that we are not going into right now. I personally think that another plausible reason is that God wanted Priscilla and Aquila there in Ephesus to help establish the church. The problem was not that Paul wanted to go to Ephesus, he just had to pick them up first.
  • We can already see the impact Priscilla and Aquila had on Paul — he writes about them later, saying they've helped him so much in the ministry.

Part 1 — The Apollos Story
1. A New Hotshot Arrives (~30 sec)
Acts 18:24-25
  • Apollos — gifted, eloquent, knows his Scripture cold. Very charismatic, very handsome. One real problem: his gospel has gaps.
2. Corrected Privately, Received Gladly (~1 min)
Acts 18:26-28; 1 Corinthians 3:6
  • Priscilla and Aquila take him aside privately rather than correct him in public — the more honorable route culturally. He doesn't get defensive. He takes it, and goes on to become an effective, respected teacher in Corinth.
  • What a great team.
3. Was Priscilla the Lead Teacher? (~1.5-2 min)
Acts 18:26
  • Name order in the text — Priscilla first — is the same convention Luke uses elsewhere: "Barnabas and Saul" flips to "Paul and Barnabas" right when Paul steps into leadership (Acts 13:43, then it goes back and forth). Applied here, it suggests Priscilla was at least an equal co-leader in this correction — maybe the primary one.
  • Historically this was even more notable than it sounds — traditionally, if a man and woman were named together, the man was named first by default. So naming Priscilla first is a real, deliberate signal that she had a prominent role teaching Apollos.
  • The "Dave" bit: think of a group project at work, presenting slides to the boss, and your coworker Dave's name is listed first. You ask Dave why. "Oh, I didn't notice, I just made the slides, it's fine, we all worked on it equally." So you ask Dave to put your name first instead — "No, I made the slides, my name's first." Someone else says "let's just do it alphabetically" — and you say "Alan Adamson, I don't want to hear it." Everyone argues, because name order matters. It communicates honor and credit for contribution. Name order still matters today.

9. Her Greek Word Choice (~1-1.5 min)
1 Timothy 2:12
  • "Exercise authority" translates αὐθεντεῖν (pronounced ow-then-TEEN), a rare word that elsewhere can carry a sense as aggressive as "dominate" or "usurp" — this may be a specific, virulent kind of teaching Paul's shutting down, not teaching itself.
  • I told you at the beginning I'm more of an egalitarian mutualist — I think women should teach. But if any of them are yelling to the point of intensely, almost verbal-assault-murder-level intensity, I'd tell them to take a break too.
10. "It Takes Two... to Quarrel?" / "Dude, Chill Out" (~1 min)
1 Timothy 2:8
  • Zoom out, and it doesn't look like a women-only problem. Just before this (1 Timothy 2:8), Paul tells the men to pray "without anger or quarreling" — sounds like the whole Ephesian congregation was unruly, not just the women in it.
  • These men are praying and arguing with such intensity, Paul's thinking, "wow, it's like Armageddon in here — I'm feeling the wrath of God raining down. I thought we were worshiping, I thought we were having communion. What's happening in Ephesus, seriously?"
11. What Was Going On In Ephesus (~1 min)
  • Slide has two images: a WWE wrestler smacking another guy with a chair, and a nice glass of wine being poured. This beat now pivots from the church-tone thread into the cultural/Artemis thread, rather than closing both out.
  • It makes some of Paul's other instructions to Timothy make more sense too. He says, "let no one despise you for your youth" (1 Timothy 4:12). It's almost like everyone there is just throwing knuckles — women yelling mid-teaching, men yelling at each other during prayer, and nobody's listening to Timothy because he's just a kid in their eyes. Paul's basically saying: you're the leader in that room, Timothy — don't let anyone look down on you. Give them the chair, Timothy. (wrestler image)
  • And it makes sense of 1 Timothy 5:23 too — Paul telling Timothy, "you know what, it looks like you could use a little wine — just relax a little bit." (wine image)
  • So what was actually driving all this tension? There's real cultural water this Ephesian church was swimming in — let's look at it.
12. Patron Goddess: Artemis (~1 min)
  • Ephesus's Artemis wasn't one god among many — ancient sources rank her the second-most-titled deity in the entire Greek pantheon, behind only Zeus. Pausanias himself wrote: "all cities worship Artemis of Ephesus, and individuals hold her in honor above all the gods" (Pausanias, Description of Greece 4.31.8). (Slide shows the ranking: Zeus 67, Artemis 64, Athena 59, Apollo 58, Aphrodite 27, and so on.)
  • Her temple: four times the size of the Parthenon, 127 sixty-foot columns — one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.
  • This wasn't just religious devotion, it was civic pride — when Paul's preaching threatened the cult, the city rioted: "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" (Acts 19:28)
  • Her domains — hunter, moon, celibacy, childbirth, vengeance — actually connect back to the other 1 Timothy 2 threads we're NOT going deep on today (hair, Eve, childbearing). (Tease the ref doc again here.)
  • This is the cultural water this church was swimming in — worth keeping in mind for what's next.
13. Competitive Women in the Cult — Climbing the Priestess Ladder (~45 sec)
1 Timothy 2:12
  • There weren't a lot of job opportunities for women back then — no corporate ladder. The closest thing was the cult of Artemis: women came from all over the Mediterranean to become priestesses. It was honorable, reputable, well-paying — and it looks like it was very competitive. The way you got ahead was by taking the priestess above you down.
  • Read aloud:
    "The learning setting as illustrated in Ephesiaca for women in Ephesus is not depicted as quiet, and women do not take a submissive posture... they take a fiercely competitive rather than humbly submissive posture to vie for different priestess roles."— Gary Hoag, Wealth in Ancient Ephesus and the First Letter to Timothy
14. Wealthy Women Patrons — More Donations = More Power (~45 sec)
1 Timothy 2:12
  • Worth a real comparison here — wealthy women acted as patrons to both the cult AND the church. Lydia, the wealthy dealer of purple cloth in Philippi, hosted a church in her home (Acts 16:14-15). Jesus's own ministry was bankrolled in part by women of means (Luke 8:1-3). The church really did benefit from wealthy women investing in it.
  • The Artemis cult was also famous for its wealthy female benefactors — real titles: priestess, prytanis, gymnasiarch. But in the cult, wealth converted directly into power and authority. In the early church, it wasn't supposed to — wealth was never meant to buy authority.
  • If a wealthy woman was used to her giving buying her authority in one system, she may have tried the same leverage in the other.
  • What this is all building to: the women of Ephesus — rich or not, powerful or not — had grown used to a culture where if you can take down the woman above you, or give enough money, you should have more say, you should be running the show. That's how it worked in the cult of Artemis. Why wouldn't it work here too? And Paul is saying: no, that's not how the church works.
  • Cut from live talk: the magic/"busybodies" angle (1 Timothy 5:13) — take-home reference only.
15. New Building, Same Hustle — Cutthroat Habits Don't Stop at the Door (~20-30 sec)
  • Short landing beat, wrapping the whole Artemis-cult thread before moving on — the priestess ladder and the wealth-for-power habit didn't stay behind at the temple. Same women, new building, same instinct.

16. Let's Back It Up — Emulate the Men (~2-2.5 min)
Acts 18:26; 1 Timothy 2:9-15
  • One slide covers all of this now — no separate Aquila/Apollos image slide. This slide is doing double duty: resolving the 1 Timothy tension AND landing the emulation beat.
  • That changes how we read the passage in Timothy a bit, doesn't it? Maybe not completely — scholars still debate Ephesus-specific vs. universal. If you're still not sure how to land this passage, go back to the story we just watched: how did the men handle it?

Segment 3 — Be Unified  (~14.75-15.75 min — timed from an actual full practice run in this order, then trimmed the slavery example down to one live sentence, saving ~30-45 sec)
"Let's bring it back. We've been talking about marriage the way God designed it — two pieces: working together as a team to build God's kingdom, and being unified with our partners. That's really important — last week in Ephesians we talked about how Jesus came down to unify us with God. I think Priscilla and Aquila, in addition to modeling teamwork well, modeled unity well too."
1. Priscilla & Aquila — Till Death Do Us Part (~1-1.5 min)
  • They are always together, and they lived a lot of life in a lot of places.
  • Named six times in the New Testament — always in the same verse, never once apart.
  • A lot of life, a lot of places, a lot of mileage on that marriage: Pontus → Rome → Corinth → Ephesus → Rome → Ephesus.
  • They're always together, always in the same verse, always doing God's work. They're always unified, never divided.
2. Ephesians 5:22-33 — Submit and Love (~45-50 sec)
Ephesians 5:22-33
  • Read Ephesians 5:22-33 aloud — full text on the slide. Paul calls wives to submit to their husbands, to respect their authority, and calls husbands to love their wives just as Christ loves the church.
  • This is good advice — love and respect are needed to preserve relationships. Submission is fairly straightforward: to respect the leadership, to not object to their authority.
  • Love is more nuanced — that's next.
3. What Is Love? (~35-40 sec)
  • "Baby don't hurt me" — joking aside, what is love really? Not your lovey-dovey, sappy "oh when we first met, we were in love" stuff.
  • Love is proactive. It's self-giving — the pursuit of seeking blessing, of being united with someone. "For God so loved the world that He sent His Son," so that we can be together with Him.
4. What Builds and Breaks Unity? (~55 sec-1 min)
Ephesians 5:1-2, 5:21
  • Look at the opposites of submission and love — their opposites break unity. The opposite of submission is defiance: not willing to work with the other person, inviting conflict.
  • The opposite of love doesn't seem as sinister — it's just apathy. "For God so didn't care, so He didn't do anything to help reunite with us" — that's not how the verse goes. If He didn't love us, we wouldn't have the opportunity to be reunited with Him.
  • Submission and love aren't just needed within marriage — they're critical for unity across the whole church.
5. Scriptural Tension (~50-55 sec)
Ephesians 5:1-2, 5:21
  • The tension happens within the same letter, the same chapter: Paul calls EVERYONE in the church to love and submit. Ephesians 5:1-2 — "walk in love, as Christ loved us." Ephesians 5:21 — "submit to one another out of reverence for Christ." No gender restriction on these — every Christian needs to love and submit to one another.
  • You get the tension — Paul says we all need to love and submit to one another, but within marriage, men do this one thing, women do this other thing. So how do we reconcile these?
6. Timeless vs. Cultural Truths (~1-1.5 min)
Romans 8:38-39
  • There's so much truth in the Bible, and a lot of it is timeless truth — doesn't matter when, where, or what culture or society is doing, it's true. Read Romans 8:38-39 aloud — full text on the slide.
  • This is as true today as it was 2,000 years ago when Paul wrote it — just as true in Portland, Oregon, as it was to the people of Rome. True no matter when or where it's applied.
7. How Do We Navigate These Cultural Truths? (~45 sec-1 min live — trimmed for time; full slavery example kept below for reference/handout, not delivered in full)
  • There's also a lot in the Bible that seems odd. People who challenge the Bible say it has no place in the 21st century — so outdated, so antiquated. They point to passages that seem backwards and ask: is the Bible endorsing this?
  • Live version — brief mention only: a quick nod that this same pattern shows up in tougher passages too — the Bible's instructions on slavery, for instance, were pushing that culture toward a better ethic than where it started, even though they read as barbaric by today's standard. Don't dwell on it — one sentence, then move straight into the Levirate Marriage example, which does the full teaching work live.
  • Full version (reference/handout only, not spoken at length): people say the Bible endorses slavery, and by today's standards, slavery is barbaric. But when you look at these passages in the time and culture they were written in, the instructions about slavery are pushing the boundaries of what was acceptable within that culture, toward a higher ethic — God's ethic. At the time, people would look at those instructions and think "wow, God is advocating we treat slaves better." We, in our day and age, have moved past that entirely — we recognize slavery is wrong outright, and looking back, it can look like the Bible is endorsing something we now reject, when it was actually pushing culture toward something better than what it started with.
  • This redemptive-movement framework comes from Slaves, Women & Homosexuals by William J. Webb — hold up the book. (Confirm this is the right title before printing.)
  • God is pushing people to live more in line within His kingdom, but not pushing them beyond what their culture will allow. (Echoed again on "Submitting to the Current Culture.")
8. Levirate Marriage (~1.5-2 min)
Deuteronomy 25:5-10
  • A quirkier example — something we thankfully don't do anymore. Read the passage aloud — full text on the slide.
  • What's happening here? Are we just trading wives around like property? Are we treating her like a dog — "sorry, your owner died, here's a new home that'll take care of you"? And what about the man — does he have no say? What if he didn't like his sister-in-law, what if she wasn't his type? Is the only recourse public shame and getting spat on?
  • In its original cultural context, this was purely redemptive. Widows were one of the least protected classes at the time — women couldn't provide for themselves, and society gave them little value outside of having children. A widow with no children was condemned, in every culture and society at the time, to be at the mercy of everything else.
  • This law was God protecting these women — "I have a plan to provide for you, and I want to push the culture you're in to help with that." Not needed today — women can work, provide for themselves, and have far more value in society than just having kids. The law feels outdated because it is — we don't need it anymore, and letting it go isn't in conflict with God's ethic, which was always about taking care of people who can't take care of themselves.
9. Submitting to the Current Culture (~1-1.5 min)
1 Corinthians 9:22
  • Paul is working within the existing social structure of his time — men were in charge of households, very patriarchal. When he writes to men, he assumes they have authority. Notice: he does NOT command them to lead, does NOT command them to rule. It's assumed, not ordered.
  • Callback to Athens, back in Segment 1 — Paul is an expert at making the Gospel applicable across multiple cultures. This is that same instinct at work: "I have become all things to all people, that I might by all means save some." (1 Corinthians 9:22)
  • He raises the bar within that structure: "okay, men, you're going to lead because you have to in this society and culture — but I'm going to hold you to a higher standard. You need to love your wives like Christ loves the church." That's a big ask — at the time, men could do whatever they wanted, and women were at the mercy of their husbands.
10. What Do You Need to Work On? (~1.5-2 min)
  • Nowadays, our culture has changed — women have more power, more agency. Not every marriage assumes the man is automatically the authority figure. So what does this instruction look like in our day and culture? Paul wrote that we ALL need to love and submit to one another — we all need to practice it, all need to get good at it. This is my spicy take, given Paul's been giving spicy advice all morning.
  • Scenario one: a husband reading this passage says, "Thank God I don't need to submit to my wife! I could not bear to do that." I think Paul would say: "We all need to submit, brother. Sounds like a wonderful opportunity to grow and practice submission."
  • Scenario two: a wife says, "I'm okay submitting, but I really don't care about him, I'm withdrawing from this relationship." I think Paul would also challenge her: "I know it can be difficult, but Christ pursued you, seeks to bless you, wants to be with you and prosper you — you need to do the same for your husband."
  • We all need to work on love and submission — our marriages are a good opportunity to do that.
11. Back to Priscilla & Aquila — Unified Even When It Is Not Easy (~1.5-2 min)
Acts 18:1-4; 2 Timothy 4:19
  • We know they were unified in their marriage, always together, traveled a lot — but they didn't always have an easy time. You can coast through a marriage when it's not challenging; they had real challenges.
  • Fact: they ended up in Corinth originally because they were exiled — the emperor didn't want Jews in Rome, part of a mass exodus, trying to find a new place in a new city because they weren't wanted where they were from. (Acts 18:1-4)
  • Callback to the journey: from Ephesus to Rome, then back to Ephesus. Notice — by 2 Timothy, in Ephesus the last time, they're no longer hosting a home church. They'd hosted one everywhere else they went, but not this time.
  • Theory, not fact — slide marks it with a "?": some scholars think they may have lost their home in the fire of Rome, AD 64 — 10 of 14 districts burned, and a lot of people lost everything. It's possible they had money, a house, provisions in Rome, but lost it all and went back to the friends and church they knew in Ephesus, needing to be taken care of. More detail in the reference materials if anyone wants to dig in — this traces to Gordon Franz's research, not settled scholarly consensus.
  • They'd always supported other people with their money — but now they needed the support themselves.
