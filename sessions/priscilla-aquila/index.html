Priscilla & Aquila: Marriage, Submission & Oneness
Priscilla and Aquila are named six times in the New Testament — always together, always in the same verse. What does a marriage that stays that unified actually require? Genesis 2:18-24 sets the frame: marriage was designed for teamwork ("a helper fit for him") and unity ("no longer two, but one flesh"). Segment 2 follows Priscilla and Aquila working as a team to correct Apollos, and reads Paul's hardest words to women in Ephesus against the city's own cultural backdrop — the cult of Artemis, and a genuinely unruly church. Segment 3 turns to marriage itself: love, submission, what builds and breaks unity, and how a redemptive-movement reading helps navigate passages that look very different today than they did in their own time and culture. Not a session aimed at settling the wider debate — aimed at becoming better partners.

The Frame — Genesis 2:18-24

"It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper fit for him... Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh." Two halves of marriage's original design: teamwork (v.18) and unity (v.24 — the same verse Jesus quotes in Matthew 19:6). Priscilla and Aquila model both. That's the lens for the whole session.
Before diving in: a fair warning that this topic can get contentious, and a set of shared safety tools — separating research from personal opinion, respecting that not every marriage looks the same, and modeling the church's own posture of staying unified even without full agreement.

Segment 1 — Meeting Priscilla & Aquila

Acts 15-18
Paul's second missionary journey was rough — a split with Barnabas, blocked by the Holy Spirit, flogged in Philippi, chased out of two cities, mocked in a third. He arrives in Corinth alone and worn down, into a city with the ancient world's worst reputation.
  • The turnaround — Paul finds Aquila and Priscilla, Jewish tentmakers who are already Christians. He stays with them, settles for a full year, and builds up the Corinthian church. (Acts 18:1-3)
  • Who they really were — more than average tentmakers: they hosted churches in their own home, traveled constantly (Pontus → Rome → Corinth → Ephesus → Rome → Ephesus), and quite possibly represented a mixed marriage across a real class line — Aquila explicitly Jewish, Priscilla's name tied to a Roman noble family.
  • On to Ephesus — Paul brings them along, stays briefly, and leaves them to help establish the church there. Already, his own letters show the impact: he later writes that they've helped him so much in the ministry.
Table Discussion — Round 1
Has God ever sent you a "Priscilla and Aquila" — someone who showed up right when you needed support most?

Segment 2 — Working as a Team

Acts 18:24-28; 1 Timothy 2
The hottest segment of the day — given the time and respect it deserves rather than rushed past.
  • Correcting Apollos — a gifted, popular young teacher arrives in Ephesus with real gaps in his gospel. Priscilla and Aquila take him aside privately and correct him, together — and the way Luke writes their names (Priscilla first, more often than not) mirrors his own convention for signaling who's actually leading.
  • The hard passage — "I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man" (1 Timothy 2:11-12). Read against Ephesus's own cultural water: a church with a real tone problem (men corrected for anger and quarreling in the very same passage), and a city dominated by the cult of Artemis, where women held real, well-documented civic and religious office — offices bought and paid for through wealthy patronage.
  • Emulating the men — Aquila didn't sideline his wife; he partnered with her. Apollos, corrected by a woman, received it gladly rather than getting defensive. Both men welcomed Priscilla to the table.
Table Discussion — Round 2
Think of a couple you know who work well together. What is their secret?

Segment 3 — Be Unified

Ephesians 5
Priscilla and Aquila lived a lot of life together, in a lot of places — always unified, never divided. What actually holds a marriage together like that?
  • Submit and love — Ephesians 5:22-33: wives submit, husbands love as Christ loved the church. Submission is fairly intuitive; love takes real definition — proactive, self-giving, not a feeling but a direction.
  • The tension — earlier in the same letter, Paul calls EVERYONE to love (5:1-2) and submit to one another (5:21), no gender restriction. So how do the marriage-specific instructions square with that?
  • Timeless vs. cultural truth — some Scripture is true across all time and culture (Romans 8:38-39); other passages need to be read within their original setting to see how they were genuinely redemptive for their own time, even when they look backward by today's standard. Levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25:5-10) is the working example — a strange-sounding law that was, in its own context, real protection for the most vulnerable people in that society.
  • Reading Ephesians 5 now — Paul works within the patriarchal culture he's writing into rather than commanding a social revolution (1 Corinthians 9:22) — he never tells husbands to rule or lead, only to love, with a steep standard of accountability. Stretching the culture, without snapping it.
  • Back to Priscilla & Aquila — their unity wasn't easy. Exiled from Rome once already, and quite possibly having lost their home a second time in the Great Fire of Rome (AD 64) — after years of supporting others, they became the ones who needed support. Through all of it, still together. No longer two, but one.
Table Discussion — Round 3
We all need to become better at love and submission. Which do you think YOU need to work on? And who is a good partner to work on this with?

Close

This may look different depending on the relationship, the situation, or the cultural context — but Paul's call to submit and love applies to everyone. Work with your spouse to figure out what unity actually requires right now, and let that unity fuel the gospel's advance through your marriage.
Priscilla and Aquila don't just illustrate marriage in general — they model both halves of Genesis's original design, teamwork and unity, lived out across exile, hardship, and decades together.

No longer two, but one.
