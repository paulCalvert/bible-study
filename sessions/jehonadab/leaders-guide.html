Leader's Guide

Jehonadab: Leaving a Legacy

Event: CMBC Men's Breakfast  |  Date: October 2026  |  Materials by: Paul Calvert
Intro — Before Prayer  (~3 min)
1. Remarkable Men of the Faith (~1 min)
  • This year we've spent our mornings with some remarkable men of the faith. We started with Jesus, the foundation for everything. Then Abraham, the father of the faith. Then Daniel. Then Nehemiah rebuilding the walls. Then Priscilla and Aquila. Remarkable, every one of them.
  • So who's next on the list? Who else belongs in that company?
  • Jehonadab. (Pause for the confusion.) Son of Rechab. Anybody? Why is this the first time we're hearing about him?
2. Why Him? (~1.5 min)
  • That's fair. He shows up in a handful of verses. What makes him remarkable isn't what he did. It's what he left behind.
  • Here's how I'm using the word "legacy" this morning:
    Legacy is what we have taught those who follow us — through intention or by accident.
  • Hold onto that "by accident" part. It's going to matter.
  • Today is about leaving a godly legacy. If you're one of the younger guys, you might just be starting to think about what you'll pass on. If you're further down the road, you might be looking back and wondering what kind of impact you've had. And if you're somewhere in the middle, you might be asking whether you're getting it right.
  • Wherever you are, every one of us can also look back at what was left for us by the people who came before.
3. Pray (~30 sec)
  • Pray, then move into Section 1.
Section 1 — David's Legacy  (~13.5 min | 1–2 Samuel)
"Jehonadab is obscure. So let's start with someone who isn't: King David."
1. The Contrast Figure (~40 sec)
1 Samuel 13:14; Acts 13:22
  • David is called a man after God's own heart. He shows us things about God, and even about Christ, that we'll dig into properly when we spend next year on David. That's not today's job.
  • David's legacy with God is remarkable. His legacy with his own family is a tragedy.
2. Michal and the Ark (~1 min)
2 Samuel 6:16, 20-22
  • David's first wife was Michal, the daughter of King Saul (1 Samuel 18:20-27). The moment most of us know her from: David brings the Ark of the Covenant into Jerusalem and dances before the Lord with everything he has. Michal watches from a window and despises him for it (2 Samuel 6:16). When he gets home, she lets him have it:
    "How the king of Israel has distinguished himself today, going around half-naked in full view of the slave girls of his servants as any vulgar fellow would!" — 2 Samuel 6:20 (NIV)
  • And David answers:
    "...I will celebrate before the LORD. I will become even more undignified than this, and I will be humiliated in my own eyes..." — 2 Samuel 6:21-22 (NIV)
3. The Usual Lesson, and Her Side (~30 sec)
  • Most of us have heard this story, and we know the usual takeaway: be like David, not like Michal. (Rhetorical. Don't take answers.)
  • I've heard more than one sermon that dunks on Michal, and there's truth there. But let's read it from her side for a few minutes.
4. Michal's Story (~2 min)
1 Samuel 18–19, 25:44, 31; 2 Samuel 2–3
  • A love story. Michal loved David (1 Samuel 18:20). Her brother Jonathan was David's closest friend (1 Samuel 18:1-4). David was the giant-killer, very likely the next king.
  • Separated. Then her father turns on David. Michal helps him escape through a window (1 Samuel 19:11-17). Saul even turns on Jonathan for defending him (1 Samuel 20:30-33). With David on the run, Saul gives Michal to another man, Paltiel (1 Samuel 25:44).
  • Grieving. Her father and three of her brothers, Jonathan among them, die in a single battle (1 Samuel 31:2-6). Another brother, Ish-bosheth, takes what's left of the kingdom, and a civil war with David drags on (2 Samuel 2:10). The man she once loved is at war with her family. Her loyalties are torn in half.
  • Reclaimed. As part of a political deal with Abner, David sends for her (2 Samuel 3:13-14). Paltiel follows her, weeping the whole way, all the way to Bahurim, until Abner tells him to go home (2 Samuel 3:15-16).
5. Happily Reunited!... Right? (~1.5 min)
2 Samuel 3:2-5; 5:13
  • Michal is walking to her first husband, David. What is she thinking? Is she excited? Is she scared? It has been somewhere between 10-15 years at this point.
  • Then she sees him. He's older now, but still handsome. BUT RUGGED. He is a man. Her heart rushes, her voice catches in her throat, and she goes to speak... WHO THE HECK ARE ALL THESE WOMEN?
    • While they were apart, David married six other women: Ahinoam, Abigail, Maacah, Haggith, Abital, and Eglah (2 Samuel 3:2-5). Michal is now wife number seven.
  • And after they're reunited, David goes on to collect MORE wives and concubines. We don't know the count, but it all happened after Michal came back to David and before the Ark returned to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 5:13).
  • Michal is now married to a man she no longer knows, competing for his attention with women who've been with him for the past decade. She's trying to figure out how she fits into this new family while her old one is dying off. And she has no one else left. Her father is dead. Her brothers are dead. She has no children. David is her only lifeline, her only connection to family. She's not looking at the man she married. She's looking at a stranger.
6. Have You Judged the Guy Next to You? (~1 min)
  • Now let's talk about worship. It's actually a pretty sensitive thing, and even in this room we may all worship a little differently.
  • Have you ever caught yourself judging the guy next to you? Maybe he's got his hands up and you're thinking, "Tone it down." Some of you remember Cedar Mill twenty or thirty years ago, when worship here was a lot more reserved. If that's all you'd known, a guy dancing in the aisle would have been a shock.
  • I specifically remember Pastor Carl saying once, half-joking, "If you feel so moved by the Spirit, you are allowed to raise your hands." He knew the culture here leaned hard against any outward enthusiasm in worship, and he was challenging it.
  • Or it goes the other way. He's standing stone-still and you're thinking, "Is he even awake?" Careful there too. Quieter worship isn't lesser worship. Judging the more reserved guy is the same mistake Michal made, just pointed in the other direction.
7. Revisiting the Ark Scene (~1 min)
2 Samuel 6:16, 20-22
  • (Slide: "Revisiting 2 Samuel 6:16, 20-22.") So let's go back to the Ark scene, knowing what we know now. A first love, ripped away. Given to another man. Her father and brothers killed. Taken back in a political deal, to a husband she doesn't know anymore, with a house full of wives who've been with him for the past decade.
  • Where is Michal coming from here? I think she's coming from a place of hurt and isolation. And worship is deeply personal. It doesn't look the same for everyone. Then she sees David worshipping in a way she can't fathom or connect with, and she lashes out. It's not right, but I can understand where she's coming from.
  • This was a moment that could have brought them closer. A chance to understand each other. So what does David do with it?
8. David's Response (~1.5 min)
2 Samuel 6:21-23
  • (Slide: "David's Response.") He responds poorly. Well, we read about him becoming "even more undignified" earlier. THAT is not a problem. Make it a poster, it's great.
  • Read David's whole response aloud:
    "It was before the LORD, who chose ME rather than YOUR FATHER or ANYONE FROM HIS HOUSE when he appointed me ruler over the LORD's people Israel — I will celebrate before the LORD. I will become even more undignified than this, and I will be humiliated in my own eyes. But by THESE SLAVE GIRLS you spoke of, I WILL BE HELD IN HONOR." — 2 Samuel 6:21-22 (NIV)
  • The problem is everything around it. He reminds her that God chose him over her father and her whole family, the family that was just killed. He tells her the slave girls' opinion of him matters more than hers. David will be honored by this, not Michal. She will be left behind.
  • The very next verse: "And Michal the daughter of Saul had no child to the day of her death" (2 Samuel 6:23). You can read it that God cursed her with barrenness because of her words. I think it's simpler, though, to read it as David and Michal never repairing their relationship.
  • Remember Priscilla and Aquila, and marriage as two becoming one? David does the opposite here. Instead of bringing growth or reunion, his response brings more division. He takes a crack in the marriage and makes it wider.
9. Stretched Past His Limit (~2 min)
  • David wasn't being cruel for its own sake. He was stretched past his limit: his own time with God, a kingdom to run, a lot of wives, a lot of kids. He had nothing left for Michal. So what he said, effectively, was: "You're on your own. Figure it out."
  • What if she'd been his only wife? Maybe he says, "I know this isn't how you're used to worshipping. What kind of worship are you comfortable with? Can we find that together?"
  • You don't need seven wives to make David's mistake. Work, hobbies, ministry, any of it can leave a man with a thriving faith of his own and nothing left to walk alongside his family in theirs.
10. The Accidental Legacy (~2.5 min)
2 Samuel 11, 13–18; 1 Kings 11:1-13
  • David told Michal to figure it out. And because of everything else pulling at his time, he likely told the rest of his family the same. The problem is that while he told them to follow God, he didn't model it for them. And what he did model taught them something else entirely.
  • Not only did David pick up a lot of wives and concubines, he ended up taking another woman through adultery, murder, and lies: Bathsheba (2 Samuel 11). THIS lesson his kids took to heart: If you want something, TAKE IT. Even if it hurts you or others, even if it takes you away from God, TAKE IT.
  • And they repeated it, worse. Amnon rapes his half-sister Tamar (2 Samuel 13). Absalom murders Amnon and later rebels against his own father (2 Samuel 13–18). Solomon picked up some of what David meant to teach about fearing God, but still ends his life chasing wealth and many wives, and the kingdom splits because of it (1 Kings 11:11-13).
  • That is David's familial legacy. Not the one he meant to leave, but it is what his children picked up.
  • You might think I'm being hard on David. And yes, I am. But that's because I don't see David here. I see myself. [Go into personal example.]
Table Discussion — Round 1
What has hurt your legacy, or is threatening to hurt it? Can anything be done to repair or change course?
Section 2 — Jehonadab's Legacy  (~16 min | 2 Kings 10, Jeremiah 35, Nehemiah 3)
"David left a conflicted legacy for his family. So let's revisit Jehonadab. What did he leave for his?"
1. A Quick Heads-Up (~15 sec)
  • Quick heads-up: you'll hear me say Jehonadab and Jonadab. Same guy. 2 Kings calls him Jehonadab, Jeremiah calls him Jonadab, and I'll probably mix them up as I go.
2. Jehu and Jehonadab (~1.5 min)
1 Kings 11:11-13; 2 Kings 9:6-10; 10:15-17, 23
  • We're now about 130 years later. King David is gone, and the kingdom is split, largely because his familial legacy fractured things. We're in the northern part, the Kingdom of Israel. Israel is having a lot of problems with idolatry, and it's the period of the prophets. That's when Jehonadab comes up.
  • We see Jehonadab only twice in the Bible (2 Kings 10:15-16, 23), and only hear him say something once. It's about 841 BC. God has commissioned Jehu to wipe out the house of Ahab, the most wicked dynasty Israel ever had (2 Kings 9:6-10). Jehu is in the middle of a bloody purge when he meets Jehonadab on the road:
    "Is your heart true to my heart as mine is to yours?" And Jehonadab answered, "It is." Jehu said, "If it is, give me your hand." So he gave him his hand. And Jehu took him up with him into the chariot. And he said, "Come with me, and see my zeal for the LORD." — 2 Kings 10:15-16
3. Two Men in the Chariot (~45 sec)
2 Kings 10:29-31; 15:12; Hosea 1:4
  • Jehu is the bigger name of the two men in 2 Kings. But Jehu's own legacy was one of bloodshed and rebellion (2 Kings 10:29-31; Hosea 1:4). His line only lasted four generations (2 Kings 10:30; 15:12).
  • But what became of the other man?
4. Meanwhile, in Jerusalem (~3 min)
2 Kings 17:5-18; Jeremiah 1:1-3; 35:1-6
  • Fast-forward over 200 years, to about 598 BC. The northern Kingdom of Israel is gone. Within about 30 years of Jehu's line ending, it burned through a string of kings and fell to Assyria (2 Kings 17:5-18). Now Babylon is closing in on Jerusalem, because Judah hasn't been following God either.
  • Here's where things stand (Slide: "Over 200 Years Later."):
    • Babylon has dominated Judah for about seven years. The first siege, in 605 BC, has come and gone, and captives have already been taken to Babylon, Daniel among them (Daniel 1:1-6). Another siege is coming.
    • It's been about 330 years since the kingdom split. Eight good kings, ten bad ones. One step forward, two steps back. And even most of the good kings left the high places standing.
    • Through all of it, God kept sending prophets to call Judah back. We know at least 15 by name (2 Chronicles 36:15-16).
    • Jeremiah has been preaching in Jerusalem for about 28 years (Jeremiah 1:1-3).
  • Then God gives Jeremiah an interesting command. He tells him to prepare wine in the temple and invite a family to come and drink: the Rechabites (Jeremiah 35:1-2).
  • Now who are the Rechabites? They're from the line of Jehonadab. Back in 2 Kings, Jehonadab is called the son of Rechab, and this family traces their lineage back to Jehonadab (Jeremiah 35:6). 2 Kings didn't give us much detail on what he was like, but we actually learn more about him from his descendants, roughly eight generations later.
  • Picture it. Babylon's armies are raiding the countryside, and things are looking bad. Then the big prophet, Jeremiah, invites you to the temple. Jeremiah has been calling the kingdom back to God for years, and it's been falling on deaf ears. He's been saying a lot of scary things. And now he's invited you and all the other grown men of your family (Jeremiah 35:3). Picture yourself going. Maybe you're walking with your dad and your brothers. Maybe at this point you're the eldest man, leading the others to the temple. You arrive, and there's a large table set with bowls of wine and cups, all laid out in front of you. And then Jeremiah says, "Come in, and drink some wine." (Jeremiah 35:4-5)
5. "We Will Drink No Wine" (~3.25 min)
Jeremiah 35:6-11
  • But no one is stepping forward. Why is the room suddenly tense?
  • The Rechabites tell us what Jehonadab commanded:
    "...Jonadab the son of Rechab, our father... commanded us, 'You shall not drink wine, neither you nor your sons forever. You shall not build a house; you shall not sow seed; you shall not plant or have a vineyard; but you shall live in tents all your days...'" — Jeremiah 35:6-7
  • Depending on your translation, it might say Jehonadab or Jonadab. It's essentially Jonathan vs. Jon in Hebrew, a full name and a short form. (More detail in the scripture reference doc.)
  • Now we know a little more about Jehonadab. We know the things that were important to him: no wine, no houses, no planting fields or vineyards, living in tents. It looks like he felt it helped him be closer to God, and it was important to him that his family experienced God in the same way.
  • They go on:
    "We have obeyed the voice of Jonadab... in all that he commanded us... But when Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon came up against the land, we said, 'Come, and let us go to Jerusalem for fear of the army of the Chaldeans and the army of the Syrians.' So we are living in Jerusalem." — Jeremiah 35:8, 11
  • Now, they had heard their forefather Jehonadab, and had lived in obedience for over 200 years. But notice there's already been SOME compromise. He told them to live outside the cities in tents, but here they are in Jerusalem. Why? Because Babylon was ransacking the surrounding countryside, and for safety they were now living within the city walls (Jeremiah 35:11). Not something they wanted to give up, but a carefully considered choice in a desperate time.
  • And now they're in a room with the most well-known (and intimidating) prophet of their day. They were clearly devout men, and no doubt knew Jeremiah was speaking for God. In a culture that put so much importance on hospitality AND on being a good guest, they're in the temple being told to drink wine by Jeremiah. As the kingdom burns around the city, this is a highly charged situation. Do you dare tell him no? Or do you tell yourself, "Eh, we already compromised on the city living. What's one more compromise?"
  • What would you do?
  • Their answer:
    "We will drink no wine..." — Jeremiah 35:6
  • Jehonadab has been dead for over 200 years, and nobody in that room ever met him. They still won't touch it.
6. God's Verdict (Acted Out) (~5 min)
Jeremiah 35:12-19
  • We're all imagining this right now. There's a lot riding on this encounter, and you just told Jeremiah no. Maybe you brace for impact, and then... he just gets up... and leaves? What do you do? (Open the room for 5–10 seconds of popcorn answers.)
  • You probably follow him out, right? (Someone may say hide, or sneak out the back.) Haha, yeah, maybe. But you're curious, right? So were the Rechabites. They follow him out.
  • Ask for ~5 volunteers. They don't need to say anything, just stand up front with you. Pick guys who'll be good sports.
  • Okay, everyone, keep imagining yourselves in the Rechabites' shoes. These guys up here with me are the Rechabites who followed Jeremiah out. He's now at the front of the temple, facing all of Jerusalem.
  • You can read Jeremiah 35:12-17 for everything Jeremiah actually said. I'm going to paraphrase it to capture the tone and emotion. Take it with a grain of salt. Consider it a lesser-known "Paul translation" of the passage.
  • (Jeremiah to Judah. Not yelling, but a strained, elevated tone.) "You FAITHLESS IDIOTS. God has sent you prophets again, and again, and again. 'Come back to me,' says the Lord. 'RETURN TO ME.' AND YOU DO NOT LISTEN. YOU DO NOT OBEY. He's told you the same thing time and again. Are you dense? I PERSONALLY have been BEGGING YOU to repent, but you STIFF-NECKED, STUBBORN MORONS JUST DON'T GET IT!" (Jeremiah 35:13-15)
  • (Gesture to the volunteers.) Remember, you're the Rechabites standing behind Jeremiah. How are we feeling? (~10 seconds of popcorn answers. Don't hang on it.)
  • Jeremiah then stops and brings up the Rechabites. (Pull the volunteer closest to you forward.) "JUDAH! You see these guys? The Rechabites here? Their forefather Jehonadab, from OVER 200 YEARS AGO, told them not to drink wine. I just asked them to drink wine, and you know what they said? THEY TOLD ME NO! Their father told them ONE TIME! ONE TIME! Not to drink wine. It only took ONE GUY telling them not to drink, AND THEY LISTENED! They actually listened AND obeyed! He didn't need ALL the prophets and repeated messages to get it into their heads. Not like YOUR THICK HEADS!" (Jeremiah 35:14, 16)
  • (Let go. Open the room for ~20 seconds.) NOW how are we feeling? (If the volunteers start drifting back to their seats:) "HOLD ON, RECHABITES, I'M NOT DONE WITH YOU YET."
  • How does this end? It ends with judgment on Judah. By now they're used to hearing this from Jeremiah. They're probably thinking, "Yawn. You preach doom on us every Tuesday. Whatever."
  • "You say God needs to try harder to get you to listen. That it's too hard to obey. These Rechabites show you that YOU ARE WITHOUT EXCUSE! So here's what's coming: every disaster I've warned you about. Babylon WILL come. This city will be torn down. This temple will be destroyed. And you will be carried off and scattered far from this land. Because God called, and you did not answer!" (Jeremiah 35:17; 25:8-11)
  • (Turn dramatically to the volunteers, finger outstretched.) "AND AS FOR YOU!" (They might jump.) Now, I'm sure the Rechabites were strong, courageous men. Me? I'd have squeaked and hidden behind the bigger guy in front of me.
  • "AS FOR YOU, GOD BLESS YOU. You have honored your forefather Jehonadab and kept his commands. God says there will always be a man in Jehonadab's house to serve Him. GOD BLESS YOU." (Jeremiah 35:18-19)
  • (Thank and dismiss the volunteers.)
7. Jerusalem Falls (~45 sec)
2 Kings 25:1-21; Lamentations
  • Jerusalem does fall to Babylon, in 586 BC (2 Kings 25:1-21). And it's every bit as bad as Jeremiah had been warning them all along. If you want to know how bad, read Lamentations.
  • But what of the Rechabites?
8. They Persist! (~1.5 min)
Nehemiah 3:14
  • Remember Nehemiah from August? About 140 years after Jerusalem falls, the Jews are back and trying to rebuild the walls. Look who shows up on the work crew:
    "Malchijah the son of Rechab, ruler of the district of Beth-haccherem, repaired the Dung Gate. He rebuilt it and set its doors, its bolts, and its bars." — Nehemiah 3:14
  • The Rechabites outlasted the fall of Jerusalem!
  • But what about today? In 1836, a missionary named Joseph Wolff spent six days with a tribe near Sana'a, in Yemen, who called themselves the sons of Jonadab. No wine, no vineyards, no sowing, living in tents, and still quoting his words.
  • Roughly 2,700 years after a ride in Jehu's chariot, someone was still living the way Jehonadab taught.
  • After that, the trail grows cold. But I think there's still someone out there in the house of Jehonadab, serving the Lord in this way.
Table Discussion — Round 2
Think of the legacy left for you. Did it help you follow God, or make it more difficult?
Section 3 — Jesus's Legacy  (~16.5 min | Matthew 6, Luke 5, Matthew 26, Matthew 4)
"David was a great man, but it didn't go beyond him. Jehonadab was a no-name, and his family followed him for centuries. Why?"
1. Getting It to Stick (~1 min)
  • (Slide: "Jesus's Legacy: How we get things to stick.") So here's the question. David was a great man, a man after God's own heart. But it didn't go beyond him. His kids didn't pick up his faith. They picked up his worst habits.
  • Jehonadab is a no-name guy who shows up in a handful of verses. And his family followed him, literally, for centuries after his death.
  • Why? How did Jehonadab get it to stick, when David couldn't?
  • As a father of two boys, that's something I want to understand. And to figure it out, we're going to look at the One who left the greatest legacy of all: Jesus.
2. What I Said Last Time (~1.5 min)
Matthew 6:5-6
  • (Slide: "The Pushback: Show or Hide?") When I taught on Jehonadab before, I said that he didn't just tell his kids what to do. He showed them. It's what made sense to me at the time. So I said we shouldn't just tell our kids how to pray, for example. We should also show them how to pray.
  • But surprisingly, I got a lot of pushback when I said this. Guys cited back to me that Jesus told us to HIDE when we pray (Matthew 6:5-6). The men in that group felt we needed to keep our personal faith hidden, even from our children, because according to Jesus we'd be bragging if we made it too obvious.
  • I found this fascinating, and I've had a lot of time to think about it. I sometimes get things wrong, and I want to be open to constructive feedback. After thinking, praying, and reading on it, I think we were BOTH right.
  • Let's get into it.
3. What Jesus Actually Said (~1.5 min)
Matthew 6:1-18 (esp. 6:5-6)
  • Let's look at what Jesus said about prayer specifically, and why He said it. He talks about giving and fasting the same way, but let's focus on prayer for now.
    "And when you pray, you must not be like the hypocrites. For they love to stand and pray in the synagogues and at the street corners, that they may be seen by others. Truly, I say to you, they have received their reward. But when you pray, go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret. And your Father who sees in secret will reward you." — Matthew 6:5-6
  • He's pointing at how the Pharisees pray, and telling His disciples NOT to do that.
  • What's interesting, though: it's not tied to a curse or anything really bad. He just says their goal was to get the praise of men, and they got it. Ultimately, Jesus says it's better to get the praise of God. But if their goal was worldly praise? Congrats. They got it.
  • So when it comes to prayer, Jesus is saying that what you set out to accomplish should shape HOW you do it.
4. The Goal Determines the How (~2 min)
  • (Slide: grid. Third row starts as "???".)
    GoalHow?
    Get praised by menPray loudly in public and draw lots of attention to yourself
    Grow closer to GodPray privately and often
    Teach others to pray???
  • The Pharisees did get their reward. But Jesus is saying that if your goal is the praise of God, and a relationship with Him, you need to do something different from them: do it in secret, and do it often (Luke 18:1).
  • But notice that teaching others to pray has a DIFFERENT goal. Which means HOW we do it will be different too.
  • (Slide transition: "???" becomes the answer.)
    Teach others to prayTeach and model prayer for them (Discipleship)
  • That word is the answer to our question: discipleship. Not just telling people what to do. Not just doing it yourself where no one can see. Inviting others in to do it with you.
  • And notice what Jesus does in the very same passage. Right after "go into your room and shut the door," He says, "Pray then like this," and teaches them the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9-13).
5. Growth Is Important (~1.5 min)
Mark 1:35; Luke 5:16; 6:12; 18:1
  • (Slide: "Growth is Important.") But don't hear me saying private prayer doesn't matter. Jesus did go off by Himself to pray, and He did it often. "But he would withdraw to desolate places and pray" (Luke 5:16). He got up before dawn to do it (Mark 1:35). Before He chose the twelve, He prayed all night (Luke 6:12).
  • And He taught His disciples the same: that they "ought always to pray and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1).
  • Discipleship doesn't replace your own time with God. It depends on it. To help others grow, you have to keep growing yourself.
6. He Invited Them In (~3 min)
Matthew 26:36-46; Luke 22:39-46
  • (Slide: "Gethsemane: He invited them in.") But there were also times He invited the disciples into prayer with Him. On the night He was arrested, He brought them to Gethsemane, took Peter, James, and John in close, and went just a stone's throw farther to pray (Matthew 26:36-37; Luke 22:41).
  • He tells them why: "Watch with me" (Matthew 26:38). I always read that as Jesus wanting encouragement, and that's partly true. He wanted them with Him. But "watch" means stay awake, stay alert. Stay here with me.
  • And I suspect Jesus knew they wouldn't be much help. On the walk there, He'd just told them, "You will all fall away because of me this night. For it is written, 'I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered'" (Matthew 26:31). He'd like their company, but He knew this was something He'd have to do on His own. Having the sheep nearby might be a comfort, but they couldn't carry this. It was better for the sheep to stay near the shepherd for as long as they could, for their sake.
  • Now notice what He doesn't do. He never asks them to pray for Him. And He doesn't send them off to find their own private spots to pray. He tells them: if you pray, pray for yourselves, that you won't fall into temptation in what's about to come (Luke 22:40; Matthew 26:41).
  • So if they're awake, close by, and not praying for Him, what are they doing? They're watching Him. And He isn't being subtle. He falls on His face. He's crying out. He's in anguish, His sweat like great drops of blood (Luke 22:44), as He implores the Father:
    "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will." — Matthew 26:39
  • This really reads like a personal prayer in a closed room. But Jesus invited these guys in with Him while He prayed it. They didn't do great. They kept falling asleep (Matthew 26:40-45). But we have His actual words, in three Gospels, BECAUSE someone was close enough to hear them.
  • And that wasn't the only prayer they heard that night. Before they ever walked to the garden, Jesus prayed out loud over them for a whole chapter (John 17). He prayed for Himself, then for them, then "for those who will believe in me through their word" (John 17:20), that they would all be one, just as He and the Father are one (John 17:21). That's us. Jesus prayed for His own legacy, out loud, in front of the men He was discipling.
  • Jesus was modeling for His disciples, more than just talking, that you can take ANYTHING to God in prayer. You can bring your emotions into it and lay it all on the line. That prayer deeply mattered to Him, and was how He connected with the Father. AND He was showing them that it's okay to share that kind of prayer with the people following you.
7. Let's Talk About Basketball (~2.5 min)
  • (Slide: "Let's Simplify: Y'all Ball?") Let's simplify. Imagine you're a great basketball player, an NBA star, and you have kids. Now let's revisit the prayer grid. Depending on your goal, your approach changes. (Slide: grid.)
    GoalHow?
    Get your kids impressed with how good you areChallenge them to a game and DUNK on them
    Get better at basketball yourselfSet aside personal time, away from your kids, to practice
    Teach your kids to love and get better at basketballInvite them in to play with you, and coach them
  • Row 1: You challenge them and dunk on them. Neither of you gets any better, but hey, now they know you're way better than them. (Sometimes you do have to remind your kids you can still hoop.)
  • Row 2: You get up before everyone else and work on ball handling. The kids are at school, so you take a break from work to practice layups. The kids are in bed, and you're texting your buddies to see who's free to shoot hoops. If you want to get better yourself, you take the time ON YOUR OWN to invest in it. And you can't coach what you can't do. To help your kids get better, you have to keep getting better yourself.
  • Row 3: You invite them in to play with you, and you coach them. That means teaching (you talk), and getting them to practice. It also means physically showing them what to do. And through all of it, you show them how much you LOVE the game. In teaching them, you're saying, "I love basketball, and this is how YOU can love it and get better at it too."
  • That's discipleship. And I think that's how Jehonadab did it. He didn't play basketball, but he'd found a playbook that helped him in his relationship with God, and he invited his kids to join him. He didn't just hand them rules, either. You can't tell your family to live in tents for the rest of their lives from inside a house. Living in tents was a whole way of life, and he lived it with them.
8. Discipleship: Not Limited to Just Family (~1.5 min)
Matthew 4:19; 2 Kings 2:3
  • (Slide: "Discipleship: Not limited to just family.") But discipleship is bigger than family. Jesus had no children of His own, and He left the greatest legacy anyone ever has. He did it by inviting people to follow Him: "Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men" (Matthew 4:19).
  • And Jehonadab's legacy may have been bigger than family too. Some scholars read the Rechabites as more than blood relatives. "Sons" in Hebrew can mean followers, like the "sons of the prophets" (2 Kings 2:3). It may have been a family-based movement: people saw how this family loved God in an idolatrous time, and wanted in.
  • So if you don't have kids, or yours are grown and gone, this is still for you. Who could you invite into the way you connect with God?
9. It's Not About the Tents (~45 sec)
  • (Slide: "It's not about the tents: It's about what works.") The important thing here isn't that we all stop drinking wine and go live in tents.
  • The important thing is that Jehonadab found something that worked for him, and brought his family (and maybe others) along with him. And his life made such an impact on them that they kept living that way for nearly 2,700 years.
10. The Legacy I Want to Leave (~1.5 min)
  • Here's the legacy I want to leave for my kids:
    • I love the Bible, and I want them to learn not only how to read it, but to love it. So I'll read to them. But I'll also work on lessons like this one in front of them sometimes. It invites disruption, but I want to start looping them into the conversation.

Close  (~6 min)
1. Back to David and Jehonadab (~1.5 min)
Jeremiah 35:18-19
  • (Slide: "Closing.") So let's go back to where we started. David was a great man, a man after God's own heart. He told his family to follow God, but he didn't put in the time to walk alongside them and disciple them. He failed to safeguard his children, and his faith didn't stick past him.
  • Jehonadab did. And here's the part I love: he double dipped. Discipling his family safeguarded them. It safeguarded their lives: the family outlasted the fall of Jerusalem and was still there to help rebuild it. And it safeguarded their faith: while the rest of Israel and Judah slid further and further from God, they held on, for centuries. But on top of that, God honored Jehonadab himself. Listen to how God words the blessing: "Because you have obeyed the command of Jonadab your father... Jonadab the son of Rechab shall never lack a man to stand before me" (Jeremiah 35:18-19). His family did the obeying, but the promise carries his name, over 200 years after he died.
  • Remember the grid? The Pharisees went after the praise of men, and that's all they got. Jehonadab went after discipling his family, and got the praise of God on top of it, through his children.
2. Our Family Poster (~1.5 min)

    Humbly serve those in our influence, and boldly leverage everything for God's glory.
  • Everything we do comes down to that, so that our kids, and others, will see that this is how we love and serve God.
  • What that looks like: hosting foster kiddos for respite care. Driving out to a friend's place at 10 PM to help fix his wheelchair. Taking a friend who's struggling out to dinner and covering the bill.
  • It's not big, grandiose stuff. But it's what I want my family to be about.
