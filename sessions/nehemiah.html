Nehemiah: Innocent as a Dove, Wise as a Serpent
How does a man stay innocent as a dove AND wise as a serpent? Two earlier kings, Jehoshaphat and Ahab, each show us only half of that picture — one all innocence, no street smarts; the other all cunning, no integrity. Nehemiah is the model who holds both at once, facing wolves outside the walls (Sanballat and Tobiah) and wolves within his own people (the Jewish nobility exploiting the poor).

Core Thread

"Behold, I send you out as sheep in the midst of wolves; so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves" (Matthew 10:16). Not a call to become a wolf, and not a call to be an oblivious sheep — a call to be sheep who see the wolves coming. Nehemiah is the model of holding both at once, first with the slide deck's own visual language: wolves outside the walls, and wolves inside them.

Section 1 — Two Kings of Two Opposing Kingdoms

~15 min · 1 Kings 21–22
Before Nehemiah, two other kings each show us only half the picture. Israel was one kingdom of 12 tribes under David and Solomon, then split after Solomon's death — Judah in the south, Israel in the north. Israel never had a good king; Judah's was mixed but sometimes turned back to God. Both were eventually taken captive (Israel by Assyria, Judah later by Babylon) — which is exactly where Section 2 picks up.
Jehoshaphat, the Dove
  • Genuinely good king of Judah — sent officials and Levites to teach the Law throughout Judah's cities, appointed judges charged to fear the Lord rather than man
  • Innocent as a dove: yes. Wise as a serpent: not even close
Ahab, the Serpent
  • Worst king of Israel — persecuted the prophets, promoted Baal worship, married Jezebel
  • Naboth's Vineyard (1 Kings 21): Jezebel frames Naboth, has him stoned, and Ahab simply takes his vineyard — a 48-Laws-of-Power operator before that book existed. This is NOT the "wise as a serpent" Jesus calls us toward; it's the corrupted counterfeit
Jehoshaphat Befriends Ahab, and Doesn't Belong in the Battle
  • Jehoshaphat allies with Ahab to end conflict between the kingdoms — an admirable goal, but Ahab takes full advantage: "I am as you are, my people as your people, my horses as your horses" (1 Kings 22:4), and later marries his son Jehoram to Ahab's daughter Athaliah, sealing the alliance with a family tie.
  • God was already disciplining Ahab's kingdom through Ben-hadad's siege of Samaria (a recurring biblical pattern, e.g. Judges 2:14-15) — Jehoshaphat, already allied, wades right into the middle of it anyway.
First Red Flags, and the Major Red Flag
  • Ahab's court of 400 false prophets all say go up to battle. Jehoshaphat isn't satisfied and presses for a real one — Micaiah tells the truth (Israel scattered like sheep without a shepherd) and is thrown in prison for it. God tried warning Jehoshaphat to stay out of it, but he did not listen.
  • Ahab proposes disguising himself for battle while Jehoshaphat wears the royal robes — angling to get him killed in his place. The enemy realizes Jehoshaphat isn't the king and backs off; God saves him. Ahab dies anyway, "by accident."
Don't Be Fooled — and Closing
  • "It worked out fine" — for Jehoshaphat. Not for the people he loved most: Athaliah, his daughter-in-law, later murders her own grandchildren to seize the throne (2 Kings 11:1). Be careful of wolves — not just for you, but for those close to you.
  • Dove means trusting God with the outcome; serpent means trusting yourself enough to think, plan, and act. Jehoshaphat trusted God only; Ahab trusted himself only — both saw half the picture. Crossing-the-street analogy: trust God to help keep you from getting hit by a car, and still look both ways.
Table Discussion — Round 1
Are you more of a dove? Or a serpent?

Section 2 — Wolves on the Outside

~13 min · Nehemiah 2, 4, 6
Hundreds of years after Jehoshaphat, Israel is besieged by Babylon — walls and temple destroyed, Jews taken into exile. Persia later conquers Babylon and lets the Jews return home; the temple is rebuilt under Ezra, but the city walls are still in ruins. Nehemiah — cupbearer to the king of Persia, an actual trusted advisor at the highest level of the empire, not a lowly servant — returns to rebuild them.
Who Are the Wolves?
  • Sanballat (governs Samaria) and Tobiah (governs Ammon) — regional governors, not foreign kings, confirmed even outside the Bible in the Elephantine papyri. It displeased them greatly that someone had come to seek Israel's welfare (Nehemiah 2:10) — they want Israel weak for their own gain, straight out of the 48 Laws playbook, though compared to real imperial politics they're small-time operators.
Does Not Befriend Wolves
  • They mock him and the workers — he doesn't retaliate, prays to God instead (Nehemiah 4:4-5). They set traps to ambush him — fake meeting invitations and a false-prophet trick to lure him into the temple — he ignores them and says go away (Nehemiah 6:3-4). They start a rumor of rebellion — he doesn't argue, just says "they're making it up" (Nehemiah 6:8).
  • Not every attack needs a response — some are better off ignored.
Defense Against Wolf Attacks, and the Closing Callback
  • When they plot an actual attack (Nehemiah 4:7-9), Nehemiah responds fully: brings outside people into the city for safety, arms the civilians, and keeps a persistent vigil — half the people stand guard, half work, and even those working keep a weapon in hand, ready to drop their tools and fight (Nehemiah 4:16-18). Not looking for a fight but prepared for one.
  • He never retaliates — the wall is done in 52 days (Nehemiah 6:15-16). Years later, one of Jerusalem's own priestly leaders, Eliashib, welcomes Tobiah into a room inside the temple itself (Nehemiah 13:4-9) — Nehemiah finds out and throws his belongings back out. That threat is managed better on the outside of the walls. Callback: Jehoshaphat welcomed a wolf into his family. This time, the wolf gets thrown back out.
Table Discussion — Round 2
Do you know who the wolves in your life are? How are you treating them?

Section 3 — Wolves on the Inside

~15 min · Nehemiah 1–2, 5
Sometimes the wolves aren't outside the walls. Sometimes they're your boss — or even family members.
Wolf Boss: Artaxerxes
  • Not a soft king — took the throne by crushing his father's assassin and rival brothers, then put down Egypt's revolt and his own top general's revolt (who actually won on the battlefield, forcing Artaxerxes to swallow his pride and win him back with a formal apology instead). A real wolf.
  • Nehemiah takes 4 months to prepare in prayer before approaching him (Nehemiah 1:1; 2:1), then shows real vulnerability in his grief over Jerusalem — dangerous in front of a king like this, but their personal relationship allowed it.
  • When the king asks what he needs, Nehemiah's preparation has him instantly ready with a timeline, letters, a timber request, and a role (Nehemiah 2:7-9) — those same letters protect him later against the rebellion accusation. Preparedness is what faith looks like when you expect God to say yes.
Wolf Brothers: Jewish Nobility
  • Should be living like sheep among their own people — instead, four economic pressures hit the poor at once: famine, the king's tax, city rebuilding pulling people from paid work, and the nobles' own predatory interest (roughly 12% a year, exorbitant for the time), with families giving their own children into slavery just to keep up.
  • It comes to a head at Nehemiah 3's beautiful cross-class labor — nobles and common laborers building the wall side by side — except you're working next to the very man who holds your debt, or who owns your kid.
Calling Wolf Brothers Out to Be Sheep
  • Nehemiah is angry and goes to God (Nehemiah 5:6). He calls a public assembly rather than a private word, because this was a community problem — he doesn't let them hide in secret (5:7). He compares the nobles to the surrounding nations, who actually ARE wolves: "We, according to our ability, have redeemed our Jewish brothers who have been sold to the nations, but even you sell your brothers, that they may be sold to us!" (5:8). He makes them swear an oath before the whole community AND the priests (5:12-13) — and he was prepared in advance by his own conduct, having refused to personally tax or exploit the poor even though it was his right (5:14-15).
  • Good standing prepared him to directly correct his brothers' behavior.
Table Discussion — Round 3
When we work with wolves we need to be prepared. Is there something you are waiting on God for that you can prepare for? Or is there some sin in your life that will get in the way of correcting a brother?

Close — Live Like Sheep, But Not as Dumb as Them

  • Two kingdoms, Judah and Israel — the two we all still live inside. God's Kingdom runs on dove rules; the world's runs on serpent rules (the 48 Laws playbook). Live by the Kingdom's rules and conflict with the world's isn't a maybe — expect it, and be ready for it.
  • Jehoshaphat wasn't ready — he assumed good faith from a man who had none, and his family paid for it generations later. Nehemiah was ready — he kept his integrity fully intact AND deftly handled every wolf that came for his sheep, whether it came from outside (Sanballat, Tobiah) or from within his own people (the nobles).
The world says the only way to survive wolves is to become one. Nehemiah proves that's not true — he beat every wolf here without ever touching their playbook.

Send-off: this week, walking among wolves — are you ready to stay a sheep, and still protect your own?
