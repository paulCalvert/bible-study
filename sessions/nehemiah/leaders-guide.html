Leader's Guide

Nehemiah: Innocent as a Dove, Wise as a Serpent

Event: CMBC Men's Breakfast  |  Date: August 2026  |  Materials by: Paul Calvert
Core Thread How does a man stay innocent as a dove AND wise as a serpent? Nehemiah is our model — a man who held both at once, with wolves outside the walls (Segment 2) and wolves inside his own community (Segment 3). Two earlier kings, Jehoshaphat and Ahab, each show us only half of that picture (Segment 1) before Nehemiah shows us the whole thing.
Intro — Before Prayer  (~5 min)
1. Opener — Hawaii / Bryan Haggling (~1 min)
  • Catch the room up: just back from vacationing in Hawaii last week.
  • Tell the Bryan (6yo) haggling story — a 6-year-old already working an angle for his own interest is worldly wisdom in miniature, showing up before he could even name it.
  • Sets up the 48 Laws point that follows.
2. The Verse (~30 sec)
Matthew 10:16 — "Behold, I send you out as sheep in the midst of wolves; so be wise as serpents and innocent as doves."
3. 48 Laws of Power (~1 min)
  • Bring up 48 Laws of Power (Robert Greene) — a well-known book, basically a manual for how the world pursues and holds power: take advantage of people, never fully trust anyone, lie and manipulate when it serves you, press every advantage you can get.
  • That's the way of the world. In a sense, that's "serpent" wisdom gone wrong — cunning entirely in service of yourself, with no regard for who gets hurt along the way.
  • In a phrase: "The first will be first." It calls us to be like wolves.
4. The Kingdom Runs the Other Way (~1 min)
  • When Jesus describes the Kingdom of God, He's describing a completely different economy of power — one that runs in the opposite direction of a book like that.
  • In God's Kingdom we're called to be doves: innocent, trusting, submitted to His way of doing things instead of working our own angle.
  • The reversal: "The first will be last" (Matthew 19:30). We're called to be like sheep.
5. So Why "Wise as Serpents" Too? (~1 min)
  • Is Jesus telling us to go run some of those plays ourselves — take advantage of people, angle for our own advancement? Not at all.
  • He's telling us to be wise to the schemes of people who do live by those rules — to see it coming, to not be naive to it, to be prepared for it — without ever adopting those tactics ourselves. In the verse we read earlier, Jesus is calling us to be sheep, NOT to be wolves. He is instead telling us how to live among wolves.
6. Preview — Then Pray (~30 sec)
  • Of all the men in the Bible, I can think of no better example in modeling this than Nehemiah. He was able to live as a citizen of God's Kingdom, keeping his innocence, but was wily enough to avoid danger when working with wolves.
  • Pray, then move into Segment 1.
Segment 1 — Two Kings of Two Opposing Kingdoms  (~15 min | 1 Kings 21–22)
The best way to talk about Nehemiah in this context... is to start with NOT Nehemiah! Let's look at two other men in the Bible who represented opposing kingdoms and had experience with one another. Keep this part light — it's the setup, not the main event.
1. The Setup — One Kingdom, Then Two (~2 min)
  • Israel was originally one unified kingdom of 12 tribes under David (and then Solomon). After Solomon's death, it split into two kingdoms.
  • Judah (the southern kingdom) had a mixed record — some good kings, some bad — but at least sometimes turned back to God.
  • Israel (the northern kingdom) never had a good king. Not once, across its entire history, did a king of the northern kingdom turn the nation back to God.
  • Israel was eventually taken captive by Assyria; Judah later taken captive by Babylon. (That exile is where Segment 2 picks up.)
  • This is all happening well before that exile — two kingdoms existing side by side, sometimes at odds, sometimes allied. That's the world we're stepping into today.
2. Jehoshaphat, the Dove (~1 min)
More on Jehoshaphat apart from Ahab: 1 Kings 22:41-50
  • Good king of Judah (southern kingdom) — one of the rare genuinely good kings, earnestly tried to bring his people back to God.
  • Innocent as a dove: yes. Wise as a serpent: not even close.
  • He was an actual good king, not just harmless: sent officials and Levites out to teach the Book of the Law throughout Judah's cities (2 Chronicles 17:7-9), and appointed judges across the land, charging them to judge in the fear of the Lord, not for man (2 Chronicles 19:5-7).
3. Ahab, the Serpent (~2 min)
Naboth's vineyard: 1 Kings 21  |  More on Ahab apart from Jehoshaphat: 1 Kings 16:29-21:29
  • King of Israel (northern kingdom), Jehoshaphat's contemporary — one of the worst kings Israel ever had, persecuted the prophets, promoted idolatry, married Jezebel.
  • Naboth's Vineyard: Ahab understood power the way the world teaches it — the same playbook 48 Laws of Power describes. He wants Naboth's vineyard; Naboth refuses to sell (it's family land, protected under the Law). So Jezebel arranges false witnesses to accuse Naboth of cursing God and the king, has him stoned to death, and Ahab simply takes the vineyard. No debate, no negotiation — manufacture a pretext, remove the obstacle, take what you want.
  • 48 Laws Callback: Ahab is basically a 48-Laws-of-Power operator before that book existed — cunning, calculating, pressing every advantage, unconcerned with who gets hurt. This is NOT the "wise as a serpent" Jesus is calling us toward. It's the corrupted, self-serving counterfeit — exactly the kind of operator being wise as a serpent is supposed to help us survive, not imitate.
4. Jehoshaphat Befriends Ahab (~1 min)
  • Jehoshaphat allied with Ahab, hoping to end conflict between the two kingdoms — an admirable goal. Problem: zero street smarts in how he pursued it — too trusting, assumed Ahab was as good-natured as he was.
  • Ahab's ask:
    "Will you go with me to battle at Ramoth-gilead?" (1 Kings 22:4)
    Jehoshaphat's answer, total and uncritical:
    "I am as you are, my people as your people, my horses as your horses." (1 Kings 22:4)
    No hesitation, no conditions — he hands over everything he has, right down to his own cavalry, without a second thought.
  • Jehoshaphat also married his son Jehoram to Ahab's daughter Athaliah (2 Chronicles 18:1) — sealing the alliance with a family tie. We'll come back to the cost of that decision.
  • Ahab saw an opportunity in Jehoshaphat's good nature and took full advantage of it. No innocence in it for him at all.
5. Jehoshaphat Does Not Belong in the Battle (~1 min)
  • God sometimes disciplines Israel and Judah by sending enemies to conquer them, so they'll turn back to Him — that's a recurring pattern across the Old Testament (Judges 2:14-15).
  • He was doing exactly this to Ahab through Ben-hadad, king of Syria, who had besieged Samaria (1 Kings 20:1). This was God's business with Ahab's kingdom — but Jehoshaphat, already allied, wades right into the middle of it anyway.
6. First Red Flags (~2 min)
1 Kings 22:5-28
  • Before the battle, Jehoshaphat — to his credit — doesn't just charge in blind:
    "Inquire first for the word of the LORD." (1 Kings 22:5)
  • Ahab gathers his prophets — about 400 of them — who all say the same thing:
    "Go up, for the Lord will give it into the hand of the king." (1 Kings 22:6)
  • Jehoshaphat isn't satisfied. He can tell these 400 aren't legitimate prophets of the LORD — they're not even claiming to speak in Yahweh's name. He presses further:
    "Is there not here another prophet of the LORD of whom we may inquire?" (1 Kings 22:7)
  • This should have been the flag. Ahab's entire prophetic court is fake — a wolf surrounded by yes-men, not a shepherd seeking truth. Jehoshaphat senses it enough to ask the question, but doesn't act on what it's telling him about the man he's allied with.
  • Ahab: there's one more — Micaiah son of Imlah — "but I hate him, for he never prophesies good concerning me, but always evil."
  • Micaiah is sent for. First, mockingly:
    Ahab: "Micaiah, shall we go to Ramoth-gilead to battle, or shall we refrain?" Micaiah: "Go up and triumph; the LORD will give it into the hand of the king." (1 Kings 22:15)
  • Ahab knows he's being mocked and presses him:
    "How many times shall I make you swear that you speak to me nothing but the truth in the name of the LORD?" (1 Kings 22:16)
  • So Micaiah tells the truth:
    "I saw all Israel scattered on the mountains, as sheep that have no shepherd." (1 Kings 22:17)
    — Ahab will die, and the army will be scattered.
  • Ahab, to Jehoshaphat:
    "Did I not tell you that he would not prophesy good concerning me, but evil?" (1 Kings 22:18)
    For telling the truth, Ahab has Micaiah thrown in prison:
    "Put this fellow in prison and feed him meager rations of bread and water until I return in peace." (1 Kings 22:27)
    A real prophet gets jailed for honesty — a wolf punishing truth.
  • God tried warning Jehoshaphat to stay out of it, but he did not listen.
7. Major Red Flag (~2 min)
1 Kings 22:29-33
  • Ahab proposes the plan outright:
    "I will disguise myself and go into battle, but you wear your robes." (1 Kings 22:30)
    Ahab knows the enemy will target whoever looks like the king of Israel — angling to get Jehoshaphat killed in his place.
  • Jehoshaphat does as he is asked and goes into battle dressed as Ahab.
  • The enemy does go after Jehoshaphat, but when he cries out they realize he isn't the king of Israel and back off. God saves him. Ahab dies anyway — struck "by accident" — still God's providence.
8. Don't Be Fooled (~2 min)
  • "Okay, Jehoshaphat was foolish — but God saved him, he died in his own bed, seems like it worked out fine." It worked out fine for him. It did not work out for the people he loved most.
  • His foolish move wasn't just riding into battle with Ahab — it was tying his own family to Ahab's through Jehoram and Athaliah. When Athaliah's own son (king of Judah) later died, she murdered her own grandchildren to seize the throne herself (2 Kings 11:1) — nearly wiping out the entire line of David — and ruled illegitimately for six years until finally executed in a coup.
  • Be careful of wolves — not just for you, but for those close to you.
9. Closing — Trust God AND Trust Yourself (~2 min)
  • Innocent as a dove means trusting God — always right, He can be trusted with the outcome. Wise as a serpent means trusting yourself enough to actually think, plan, and act. The trap: it feels more spiritual to only do the first one.
  • Jehoshaphat trusted God only... and stopped there. Ahab trusted himself only... and stopped there. Not a partial win each — both show us half the picture.
  • Crossing-the-street analogy: I trust God to help keep me from getting hit by a car. I also look both ways before I cross the street. Trusting God was never supposed to replace using your head — it works alongside it. Don't refuse to cross out of fear — but still look both ways.

Are you more of a dove? Or a serpent?
Segment 2 — Wolves on the Outside  (~13 min | Nehemiah 2, 4, 6)
1. Intro (~2 min)
  • Hundreds of years after Jehoshaphat, Israel is besieged by Babylon — the walls and temple are destroyed, and the Jews are taken into exile to Babylon.
  • Persia later conquers Babylon and lets the Jews go back home.
  • The temple is rebuilt (under Ezra).
  • But the walls are still destroyed and the city is still in ruins — that's where Nehemiah returns, to rebuild what's left.
2. Who Is Nehemiah? (~2 min)
  • He was the cupbearer to Artaxerxes, king of Persia — sounds like a servant's role, but it wasn't. The cupbearer tasted the king's food and wine to guard against poisoning, which meant real physical proximity and real trust at the highest level of the empire. Beyond poison control, he was an actual advisor — think less "servant," more like a trusted bartender the regulars actually open up to.
  • As a member of the court, he rubbed shoulders with real palace-level operators — he already has experience with wolves. This prepares him for his work in Jerusalem.
  • As you'll see later, he also has a bit of a temper — but handles it well.
3. Who Are the Wolves? (~2 min)
  • Sanballat and Tobiah — regional governors within the Persian Empire, not foreign kings. Sanballat governed Samaria, the province directly north of Judah. Tobiah governed Ammon, the territory east of the Jordan. Both are confirmed in extra-biblical records (the Elephantine papyri), not just the Bible.
  • They want Israel weaker for their own gain — reads straight out of the 48 Laws of Power playbook. It displeased them greatly that someone had come to seek the welfare of the people of Israel (Nehemiah 2:10) — a stronger, fortified Jerusalem is a threat to their own regional standing.
  • But given Nehemiah's experience with real imperial-court-level politicians, these two provincial governors' antics read more like small-time operators by comparison — not the sharpest he's ever dealt with.
4. Does Not Befriend Wolves (~3 min)
  • They mock him and the workers (Nehemiah 2:19-20; 4:1-3).
    • Worth naming directly: Nehemiah got angry when they did this — this isn't a man with no temper. But instead of retaliating, he took it to God:
      "Hear, O our God, for we are despised. Turn back their taunts on their own heads." (Nehemiah 4:4-5)
      That's Nehemiah asking God to curse them — an uncomfortable prayer to read out loud, but honest. Better to hand God your ugliest feelings than let them fester or fire back yourself.
    • Here's the remarkable part: God does answer this prayer — just not with the curse. The wall gets built up to half its height, "for the people had a mind to work" (Nehemiah 4:6). God's actual answer to "destroy my enemies" was "empower my people to succeed." (Dove: doesn't retaliate, prays to God instead.)
  • They set traps to try and ambush him (Nehemiah 6:2-4; 6:10-14) — four separate invitations to meet at a village in the plain of Ono, meant to harm him, plus a trick from a bribed "prophet," Shemaiah, urging him to hide in the temple where he had no priestly right to enter.
    • Nehemiah ignores them and says go away:
      "I am doing a great work, so that I cannot come down. Why should the work stop while I leave it and come down to you?" (Nehemiah 6:3-4)
      No lengthy justification, no negotiation — just a clear no and back to work. With Shemaiah, he refuses outright: entering the temple would have discredited him permanently, and the goal was his integrity, not just his safety. (Serpent: sees the trap and doesn't walk into it.)
  • They start a rumor of rebellion (Nehemiah 6:5-9) — a fifth, open (unsealed, meant to be read and spread) letter accuses Nehemiah of planning to rebel and crown himself king.
    • Doesn't argue — just says they're making it up:
      "No such things as you say have been done, for you are inventing them out of your own mind." (Nehemiah 6:8)
      (Serpent: recognizes the intimidation tactic and doesn't take the bait.)
  • Not every attack needs a response — some are better off ignored.
5. Defense against Wolf Attacks (~3 min)
  • They plot to attack (Nehemiah 4:7-9) — when mockery escalates to an actual conspiracy to attack Jerusalem, Nehemiah's response changes. This is a real threat, not noise.
    • Brings outside people into the city for safety (Dove) (Nehemiah 4:22).
    • Arms the civilians and keeps a vigilant night watch (Serpent) (Nehemiah 4:13).
    • Keeps a persistent vigil — half the people stand guard, half work, and even those working keep a weapon in hand, ready to drop their tools and fight: "trowels in hand" (Serpent) (Nehemiah 4:16-18).
  • Not looking for a fight but prepared for one.
6. Closing (~1 min)
Nehemiah 6:15-16
  • He never retaliated — but the best revenge is success. The wall is finished in 52 days, and "when all our enemies heard of it... they were afraid, for they perceived that this work had been accomplished by our God."
  • He did not go out playing with wolves. But he knew well enough to take the threat of a wolf attack seriously.
  • Later, Eliashib welcomes Tobiah into a room inside the temple itself (Nehemiah 13:4-9) — one of the Jewish leaders literally invites a wolf inside. Nehemiah throws Tobiah's belongings back out. That threat is managed better on the outside of the walls.
  • Callback: Jehoshaphat welcomed a wolf into his family. This time, the wolf gets thrown back out.
Table Discussion — Round 2
Do you know who the wolves in your life are? How are you treating them?
Segment 3 — Wolves on the Inside  (~15 min | Nehemiah 1-2, 5)
1. Transition (~1 min)
  • Sometimes the wolves are not outside the walls. Sometimes they are your boss — or even family members. Let's see how Nehemiah navigates this.
2. Wolf Boss: Artaxerxes (~4.5 min)
Nehemiah 1-2
  • Not a soft king: Artaxerxes ruled by the 48 Laws himself. He took the throne by crushing his father's assassin and eliminating rival brothers, then put down a serious revolt in Egypt (backed by Athens, under Inaros, roughly 460-454 BC) and a rebellion by his own powerful general, Megabyzus. A real wolf, not someone to be trifled with. (This backdrop also explains why he said yes to Nehemiah — a fortified, loyal Jerusalem was real strategic value to Persia as a buffer if Egypt tried again. Personal favor to a trusted cupbearer, but also self-interest.)
  • Takes time to prepare before going to the king — about four months pass between hearing the news of Jerusalem's ruin (Nehemiah 1:1, month of Chislev) and finally approaching Artaxerxes (Nehemiah 2:1, month of Nisan). (Serpent: prayer and preparation before action.)
  • Shows vulnerability — a position where showing sadness in the king's presence could be dangerous, especially with a king like this one. Nehemiah lets his grief over Jerusalem show anyway when asked (Nehemiah 2:1-2). Dangerous to be sad in front of the king, but the personal relationship allowed it. (Dove: honest, no faked composure.)
  • The king asks, "What do you need?" — and Nehemiah's preparation has him instantly ready: a timeline, letters of safe passage, a timber request, even the specific role he wants (Nehemiah 2:7-9). Those same letters of authorization protect him later — when Segment 2's opposition accuses him of plotting rebellion, he's not just offering his word against theirs; he has the king's own approval in writing. (Serpent: thought it through long before he was ever asked.)
  • Cross-reference — Luke 14:28-30: Jesus makes almost exactly this point Himself:
    "For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it? Otherwise, when he has laid a foundation and is not able to finish, all who see it begin to mock him, saying, 'This man began to build and was not able to finish.'"
    Nehemiah becomes the positive fulfillment of that warning rather than the cautionary tale — prepared enough that the mockery in Segment 2 never turns out to be justified.
  • Preparedness is what faith looks like when you expect God to say yes.
3. Wolf Brothers: Jewish Nobility (~4.5 min)
Nehemiah 5
  • These are Nehemiah's own people — Jewish nobles and officials. They should be living like sheep, not wolves.
  • On timing: this likely wasn't formally addressed until later in the project, but the pressure built throughout the wall's construction — not out of nowhere.
  • Four economic pressures on the poor, all at once:
    • Famine — families mortgaging fields, vineyards, and houses just to get grain (Nehemiah 5:3).
    • The king's tax — still due regardless, borrowed against their own property to pay it (Nehemiah 5:4).
    • City rebuilding — wall labor pulling people away from paid work they needed to survive (Nehemiah 5:1-2).
    • The nobles' interest (Nehemiah 5:7) — charging their own countrymen interest on top of everything else.
      • Nehemiah 5:11 names the rate plainly: "the hundredth part," meaning roughly 1% per month — about 12% a year. Exorbitant for the ancient world, and there was a broad, cross-cultural distaste even among pagan nations for grinding desperate people with loans like this.
      • Worst of all: families were giving their own children into slavery just to keep up with the debt (Nehemiah 5:5).
  • It came to a head via Nehemiah 3's beautiful picture of cross-class unity — nobles and common laborers literally building the wall side by side, a genuinely moving image of a community united around one purpose. But here's the rub: you're standing on the wall working next to the very man who holds your debt — or owns your kid. Mid-labor, he leans over: "Hey, you still owe me." The thing that was supposed to unite them was actively breaking community instead.
  • Echoes of Jeremiah 34:8-11: generations earlier, as Babylon besieged Jerusalem, the nobles freed all their Hebrew slaves — prompted by Jeremiah, in line with God's law. Then Babylon temporarily lifted the siege, and the moment the pressure was off, those same nobles dragged the freed people right back into slavery. God's response was fierce — He named it directly as a reason judgment was coming, and Babylon returned and finished Jerusalem off for good. God takes His people enslaving their own brothers extremely seriously, and Judah already had a recent, catastrophic precedent for exactly this sin. That's the backdrop Nehemiah is confronting head-on.
4. Calling Wolf Brothers out to be Sheep (~4 min)
  • Nehemiah is angry, and goes to God (Dove) — "I was very angry when I heard their outcry and these words" (Nehemiah 5:6).
  • Calls a public assembly, not a private word (Serpent) — because this was a community problem, not a private one (Nehemiah 5:7). He doesn't let them hide in secret.
  • Compares them to the other nations (Dove) — the nations around Israel actually ARE wolves, and God calls His people not to be like them:
    "We, according to our ability, have redeemed our Jewish brothers who have been sold to the nations, but even you sell your brothers, that they may be sold to us!" (Nehemiah 5:8)
    We were once enslaved by foreign nations — now you enslave your own brothers. God calls us to live differently than the nations around us.
  • Has them swear to the community AND the priests (Serpent) — he doesn't take a verbal promise at face value. The whole assembly says "Amen" and the nobles swear an oath before the priests, building in real accountability (Nehemiah 5:12-13).
  • Nehemiah was prepared in advance for this by his own conduct — his own integrity earns him standing to confront it.
    • He did not personally tax or exploit the poor, even though it was his right as governor — he refused his own governor's allowance and lived off his own resources instead (Dove) (Nehemiah 5:14-15). Refusing to burden the people further also meant no one could turn around and accuse him of the nobles' own hypocrisy when he confronted them.
  • Good standing prepared him to directly correct his brothers' behavior.
5. Closing (~1 min)
  • He could not control how they would each respond — the king, or the nobles — so he trusted God with that.
  • Preparation helped him in both cases: it protected him in working with the king, and it gave him a platform to speak directly to the nobles.
Table Discussion — Round 3
When we work with wolves we need to be prepared. Is there something you are waiting on God for that you can prepare for? Or is there some sin in your life that will get in the way of correcting a brother?
Close — Live Like Sheep, But Not as Dumb as Them  (~5 min)
1 Kings 22; Nehemiah 4, 5, 6
  • Back to where we started: two kingdoms, Judah and Israel — the two kingdoms we all still live inside.
    • God's Kingdom runs on dove rules; the world's runs on serpent rules (the 48 Laws playbook).
    • Live by the Kingdom's rules and conflict with the world's isn't a maybe — expect it, and be ready for it.
  • Jehoshaphat wasn't ready — he assumed good faith from a man who had none, and his sheep paid for it generations later.
    • Nehemiah was. He kept his integrity fully intact AND deftly handled every wolf that came for his sheep.
    • The ones outside (Sanballat, Tobiah) and the ones from within his own people (the nobles).
  • The world says the only way to survive wolves is to become one. Nehemiah proves that's not true — he beat every wolf here without ever touching their playbook.
  • Send-off: this week, walking among wolves — are you ready to stay a sheep, and still protect your own?
