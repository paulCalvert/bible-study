Abraham: Growing in Trust

A Lifetime of Walking with God

Scripture Reference Guide  |  CMBC Men's Breakfast  |  July 2026

All passages referenced during this morning's study, plus additional commentary notes for further reading. Take this home and dig in.

Context — Who Was Abraham?
Genesis 11:26–32 — Abram's Family and the Move from Ur
"Terah took his son Abram, his grandson Lot son of Haran, and his daughter-in-law Sarai, the wife of his son Abram, and together they set out from Ur of the Chaldeans to go to Canaan. But when they came to Haran, they settled there."
Abram did not originate the journey to Canaan — his father Terah did. But Terah stopped in Haran and settled. The family never completed the trip. This sets up the question: why did God need to call Abram a second time?
Acts 7:2–4 — Stephen's Account of the First Call
"The God of glory appeared to our father Abraham while he was still in Mesopotamia, before he lived in Haran. 'Leave your country and your people,' God said, 'and go to the land I will show you.' So he left the land of the Chaldeans and settled in Haran. After the death of his father, God sent him to this land where you are now living."
Stephen makes clear that God first called Abram in Ur — before Genesis 12. The call in Genesis 12 is the second call, given after Abram stalled in Haran for decades. Abram responded the first time, but only halfway.
Commentary Note — Why Scholars Estimate 50 Years in Haran
The Hebrew word used in Genesis 11:31 for how the family settled in Haran is yashav — not a word for a temporary camp or a waypoint. It means intentional, sustained habitation. The same word is used in Genesis 47 when Israel settled in Egypt — a stay that stretched over 400 years. The word signals that Haran was not a pause. It became home.

The timeline supports a long stay. Genesis 12:4 tells us Abram was 75 when he finally left Haran. Genesis 11:32 records that Terah died in Haran at 205. Working backwards: if Terah was 205 when he died and Abram was 75 when he left (after his father's death), Terah would have been roughly 130 when Abram was born — which fits, since the mention of Terah fathering sons at "70 years" in Genesis 11:26 most likely refers to the eldest son, not Abram specifically.

Marriage customs in the ancient Near East placed marriage in the mid-to-late teens. If Abram and Sarai married around that age, God's first call likely came while Abram was in his early-to-mid twenties — not long after marriage, when the quiet anxiety about children would have already begun. From that first call to age 75 when he finally left Haran is roughly 50 years. That is not a detour. That is a life.
The Call and the Promise
Genesis 12:1–3 — God's Call and Promise to Abram
"The Lord had said to Abram, 'Go from your country, your people and your father's household to the land I will show you. I will make you into a great nation, and I will bless you; I will make your name great, and you will be a blessing. I will bless those who bless you, and whoever curses you I will curse; and all peoples on earth will be blessed through you.'"
Five times in three verses: "I will." The entire promise rests on God's faithfulness, not Abram's performance. God was speaking directly into Abram's deepest anxiety — a man with no children and a wife who could not conceive, being promised descendants beyond counting.
Genesis 12:7–9 — Abram Moves Through Canaan
"The Lord appeared to Abram and said, 'To your offspring I will give this land.' So he built an altar there to the Lord, who had appeared to him. From there he went on toward the hills east of Bethel and pitched his tent, with Bethel on the west and Ai on the east. There he built an altar to the Lord and called on the name of the Lord. Then Abram set out and continued toward the Negev."
God promised the land to Abram's descendants — not to Abram himself. Abram was the most temporary person in Canaan and simultaneously the most permanent. He never built a house. He kept moving. And everywhere he stopped, he built an altar.
Egypt — The First Failure
Genesis 12:10–20 — Abram in Egypt
"Now there was a famine in the land, and Abram went down to Egypt to live there for a while because the famine was severe... As he was about to enter Egypt, he said to his wife Sarai, 'I know what a beautiful woman you are. When the Egyptians see you, they will say, "This is his wife." Then they will kill me but will let you live. Say you are my sister, so that I will be treated well for your sake and my life will be spared because of you.'"
Abram's first real test of trust — and he failed it. Rather than trusting God to provide, he went to Egypt and made his own plan. The plan spiraled out of control. God had to intervene to protect Sarai. A pagan king rebuked God's chosen man. And yet God remained faithful.
Genesis 13:3–4 — Back to Bethel
"From the Negev he went from place to place until he came to Bethel, to the place between Bethel and Ai where his tent had been earlier and where he had first built an altar. There Abram called on the name of the Lord."
After Egypt, Abram went back to the last place he had been right with God and started again. This is the pattern throughout his life — failure, return, restart.
Lot — Trust and Contrast
Genesis 13:8–9 — Abram Gives Lot First Choice
"So Abram said to Lot, 'Let's not have any quarreling between you and me, or between your herders and mine, for we are close relatives. Is not the whole land before you? Let's part company. If you go to the left, I'll go to the right; if you go to the right, I'll go to the left.'"
The older, wealthier man deferred to his nephew. Abram trusted God with whatever was left. This was not passive resignation — it was active trust.
Genesis 13:14–17 — God Reaffirms the Promise
"The Lord said to Abram after Lot had parted from him, 'Look around from where you are, to the north and south, to the east and west. All the land that you see I will give to you and your offspring forever. I will make your offspring like the dust of the earth, so that if anyone could count the dust, then your offspring could be counted. Go, walk through the length and breadth of the land, for I am giving it to you.'"
Immediately after Abram released the best land, God reaffirmed the promise in full. Abram's generosity did not cost him anything — it positioned him to receive more than he gave away.
Commentary Note — Lot as Contrast (Boice 482–483)
Abram had flocks but they did not have him. Lot's flocks possessed him — his wealth owned his priorities and direction. Lot started near Sodom and ended up living there fully. He told himself at each step he was in control. He was not. Proximity to sinful temptation moves inward, rarely back out. By Genesis 19, Lot sat at the gate of Sodom — a position of civic authority — with apparently zero converts. His sons-in-law laughed at his warnings. Present without impact.
The Battle and Melchizedek
Genesis 14:23 — Abram Refuses the King of Sodom's Offer
"I have raised my hand to the Lord, God Most High, Creator of heaven and earth, and have taken an oath that I will accept nothing belonging to you, not even a thread or the strap of a sandal, so that you will never be able to say, 'I made Abram rich.'"
Abram had just won a significant military victory and was offered everything he had captured. He turned it down completely. He wanted God to get the glory — and he didn't want to be indebted to a king whose character he did not respect.
Commentary Note — The Battle (Boice 495–497)
Abram was peaceful by nature but had trained 318 fighting men — prepared even when he didn't expect to need it. He did not treat Lot's capture as God's judgment on a foolish man. Lot was family and a fellow believer. Abram struck at night, dividing his force — wisdom and speed, not just courage. The eastern kings believed they had eliminated the possibility of counterattack. They were wrong.
Commentary Note — Melchizedek (Boice 501–515)
Melchizedek was king of Salem and priest of God Most High — the only figure in scripture who held both offices simultaneously before Christ. His name means "king of righteousness," and his upright life was a significant moral contrast to the kings of the plain. Hebrews 7 explains that when Abraham paid tithes to Melchizedek, the entire Levitical priesthood was "in the loins of Abraham" and paid tithes too — meaning the Levitical priesthood was subordinate to Melchizedek's order. Jesus operates as both priest and king simultaneously (F.B. Meyer) — pitying and helping (priest), commanding wind and wave (king), interceding for us (priest), reigning on the throne (king). Melchizedek is the right type because he held both offices at once. His death is not recorded in scripture, so he functions typologically as a picture of eternal priesthood. This is one of the richest theological veins in the entire Abraham narrative.
Commentary Note — Refusing the Spoils (Boice 520–525)
"Abram drew close to God before he drew away from Sodom. And since he was blessed by God, he did not need whatever blessing he might have supposed the world could give." Abram's refusal was not willpower — it was the overflow of already being full. The encounter with the King of Sodom came after Melchizedek. Abram did not require his allies to decline the spoils — discipleship demands are voluntary. Older Christians should not force younger Christians to live by standards they have not yet attained.
By Faith — The Hinge
Genesis 15:2 — Abram Questions God
"But Abram said, 'Sovereign Lord, what can you give me since I remain childless and the one who will inherit my estate is Eliezer of Damascus?'"
Abram did not demand an accounting from God, nor did he simply murmur and mutter in private. He laid the matter directly before God. God answered the honest question with the stars.
Genesis 15:5–6 — Credited as Righteousness
"He took him outside and said, 'Look up at the sky and count the stars — if indeed you can count them.' Then he said to him, 'So shall your offspring be.' Abram believed the Lord, and he credited it to him as righteousness."
This is the hinge upon which salvation swings. Not performance, not achievement, not finally getting everything right. Just belief. Paul quotes it in Romans 4. James quotes it in James 2. It is the foundation of how God saves anyone — then or now.
Commentary Note — The Covenant Ceremony (Boice 561–564)
In the ancient world, covenants were sealed by dividing animals along the backbone, placing them in two rows, and having both parties walk between the halves — swearing "may what happened to these animals happen to me if I violate this oath." In Genesis 15, God put Abram into a deep sleep and passed through the pieces alone as a smoking firepot and flaming torch. Abram did not walk. The covenant was entirely unilateral — staked on God's faithfulness alone. The smoking firepot ties to 1 Peter 1:7 (faith tested by fire) and Malachi 3:3 (God as refiner). The flaming torch ties to John 1:5. If this covenant ever fails, God himself has failed. He took that weight alone. From God's vantage point the promise was already settled — Genesis 15:18 uses the past tense: "I have given your descendants this land." Same logic as Romans 8:30.
Commentary Note — Spurgeon's Conversion (Boice 559)
Charles Spurgeon, as a young man, wandered into a Primitive Methodist chapel on a snowy Sunday. A layman was filling in — the regular preacher hadn't made it. His text was Isaiah 45:22: "Look unto me, and be ye saved." The layman preached simply on the word "look" — anyone can look, even a fool, even a child. He noticed Spurgeon struggling under conviction and addressed him directly: "Young man, you look miserable. You will always be miserable — miserable in life and miserable in death — if you don't obey my text. Look to Jesus Christ. Look! Look! Look! You have nothing to do but look and live." Spurgeon looked. That was his conversion. The same logic as Genesis 15:6 — not effort or achievement. Just looking to God and believing.
Commentary Note — The Captain Story (Barnhouse, Boice 553–554)
A rich man buys a boat, dresses as the captain, and brings his mother aboard. She says to him: "Sonny, by you, you is a captain, and by me, you is a captain — but by captains, you is no captain." Barnhouse applied it this way: "By you, you're good; by me, you're good — but by God, you're no good. In his sight you have no goodness at all." This is the point of Genesis 15:6 — righteousness is not something Abram achieved. It was something God credited to him because he believed.
Hagar and Ishmael
Genesis 16:1–4 — Sarai's Plan
"Now Sarai, Abram's wife, had borne him no children. But she had an Egyptian slave named Hagar; so she said to Abram, 'The Lord has kept me from having children. Go, sleep with my slave; perhaps I can build a family through her.' Abram agreed to what Sarai said... He slept with Hagar, and she conceived."
Sixteen years after the promise. The rationalization was layered — each layer partially true. Legal under the customs of the time. Perhaps even God's way. That is what made it so effective. The pattern runs its course: distrust → self-made solution → failure → blame.
Commentary Note — Hagar and Ishmael (Boice 569–573)
If the suggestion had come from anyone else, Abram would have refused instantly. But this was Sarai, his wife — and she had a right to do it under the customs of the time. The rationalization was layered, each layer partially true. Sarai said "The Lord has kept me from having children" — true, but with a belligerent note. When the plan failed, she blamed Abram. Scripture never records that God told Abram the name Ishmael — yet Abram named the child Ishmael, apparently convinced by Hagar's testimony that the God who had revealed himself to him so many times had now also appeared to her. A quiet moment of grace in the middle of a chapter full of failure.
The Name Change and Circumcision
Genesis 17:1–8 — God Appears, Abram Renamed
"When Abram was ninety-nine years old, the Lord appeared to him and said, 'I am God Almighty; walk before me faithfully and be blameless. Then I will make my covenant between me and you and will greatly increase your numbers.' Abram fell facedown, and God said to him, 'As for me, this is my covenant with you: You will be the father of many nations. No longer will you be called Abram; your name will be Abraham, for I have made you a father of many nations.'"
Twenty-nine years after the original call. God changes Abram's name — embedding the promise into his daily identity. Abraham means "father of a multitude." At 99, with no son by Sarai, he would walk around every day declaring a promise he could not yet see.
Genesis 17:23 — Abraham Acts Immediately
"On that very day Abraham took his son Ishmael and all those born in his household or bought with his money, every male in his household, and circumcised them, as God told him."
That very day. No delay, no negotiation. Compare this to Haran — the man who spent 40–50 years at a rest stop now moves the moment God speaks.
Commentary Note — The Name and the Breath (Boice 583–585)
The "h" added to Abram's name is the Hebrew letter heh — the sound of an exhaled breath. God was breathing himself into Abraham's name. The Hebrew name YHWH is composed entirely of vowels — it cannot be spoken without breathing. God's name is itself breath. Genesis 17:1 is also the first appearance of El-Shaddai — "God Almighty." The root shad means female breast. El-Shaddai: the all-sufficient one, the one who nourishes and sustains. Introduced specifically in the context of an impossible pregnancy. Circumcision was not a contribution to the covenant — it was a sign of it. The cutting away of flesh symbolized the renunciation of human effort and self-sufficiency. The very organ that represented Abraham's ability to produce an heir was marked with a sign that said: this is not going to happen by your power.
Commentary Note — Ishmael and the Household (Boice 590–593)
By Genesis 17, Ishmael was 13 years old. Abraham had grown genuinely proud of him. The camp had reorganized around Ishmael as the future. Into this settled dynamic, God arrived and upended everything. Ishmael was circumcised alongside Isaac — though Isaac would be the child of promise, Ishmael was not excluded from the covenant sign. God had made specific promises to Ishmael as well (Genesis 17:20).
The Three Guests
Genesis 18:1–2, 9–14 — The Visitors and the Promise
"The Lord appeared to Abraham near the great trees of Mamre while he was sitting at the entrance to his tent in the heat of the day. Abraham looked up and saw three men standing nearby. When he saw them, he hurried from the entrance of his tent to meet them and bowed low to the ground... 'Where is your wife Sarah?' they asked him. 'There in the tent,' he said. Then one of them said, 'I will surely return to you about this time next year, and Sarah your wife will have a son.' Now Sarah was listening at the entrance to the tent, which was behind him... Sarah laughed to herself... Then the Lord said to Abraham, 'Why did Sarah laugh... Is anything too hard for the Lord?'"
Abraham was sitting at the tent door — posture of active waiting. He leaped into action when the visitors arrived. He had learned to stay positioned and ready without drifting. Sarah's laughter was the laugh of unbelief. God called her out. When Isaac was born, Sarah said "God has brought me laughter" (Genesis 21:6) — the same laughter, redeemed.
Commentary Note — The Three Visitors (Boice 598–599) — Note: these notes are incomplete; additional notes pending
The three visitors are most likely Jesus (a pre-incarnate Christophany) and two angels — not the Trinity appearing as three separate figures. Two of the visitors go on to Sodom (Genesis 19:1 identifies them as "two angels"). The one who stays and dialogues throughout is identified as the LORD (YHWH). The Trinity does not appear corporally as three separate figures elsewhere in scripture.
Abraham Intercedes for Sodom
Genesis 18:20–25 — Abraham's Intercession
"Then the Lord said, 'The outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is so great and their sin so grievous that I will go down and see if what they have done is as bad as the outcry that has reached me. If not, I will know.' ... Then Abraham approached him and said: 'Will you sweep away the righteous with the wicked? What if there are fifty righteous people in the city? Will you really sweep it away and not spare the place for the sake of the fifty righteous people in it? Far be it from you to do such a thing — to kill the righteous with the wicked, treating the righteous and the wicked alike. Far be it from you! Will not the Judge of all the earth do right?'"
Abraham's intercession works because he does not ask God to make an exception — he appeals to God's own nature and God's own promises. "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" This is the model for intercession: holding God to what He has revealed about himself. Same logic as Romans 8:31–34.
Commentary Note — Intercession for Sodom (Boice 613–615)
God is not ignorant of any wickedness and not indifferent to any cry of the oppressed. The language of Genesis 18:20–21 is not God learning something new — it is God making explicit what He already knows. If God hears the cries of the oppressed and is moved to act, the question is why we don't. We have grown so accustomed to wickedness and suffering that we no longer register them as urgent. The contrast between God's sensitivity and our numbness is itself a call to repentance.
Abimelech — The Same Mistake Again
Genesis 20:1–7 — Abraham Deceives Abimelech
"Now Abraham moved on from there into the region of the Negev and lived between Kadesh and Shur. For a while he stayed in Gerar, and there Abraham said of his wife Sarah, 'She is my sister.' Then Abimelek king of Gerar sent for Sarah and took her. But God came to Abimelek in a dream one night and said to him, 'You are as good as dead because of the woman you have taken; she is a married woman.' Now Abimelek had not gone near her... Then God said to him in the dream, 'Yes, I know you did this with a clear conscience, and so I have kept you from sinning against me. That is why I did not let you touch her.'"
Twenty years after Egypt. The same bad habit. Abraham assumed the worst about these people — and turned out to be the one without moral integrity in the moment. God protected Sarah again, appearing to Abimelech in a dream before any harm was done.
Commentary Note — Abimelech (Boice 649–652)
The sin was cowardly — Abraham risked Sarah's honor to protect himself. He also slandered Abimelech: "There is surely no fear of God in this place." Abimelech actually had a great deal of reverential fear for God and a commendable sense of right and wrong. Abraham was blind to this because of his own disobedience. The irony: Abraham assumed the pagan had no fear of God, then behaved without fear of God himself. God's promises were never based on Abraham's performance. They were based on who God is. Abraham was learning that. So are we.
Isaac Is Born
Genesis 21:1–7 — The Promise Fulfilled
"Now the Lord was gracious to Sarah as he had said, and the Lord did for Sarah what he had promised. Sarah became pregnant and bore a son to Abraham in his old age, at the very time God had promised him."
Three phrases in two verses: "as he had said," "what he had promised," "at the very time God had promised." Human doubt, human schemes, human failure — none of it moved the promise or delayed the delivery. God said it. God did it. On time.
Commentary Note — Isaac's Birth (Boice 655–658)
God sent Isaac at his precise timing and Jesus at his precise timing (Galatians 4:4 — "when the fullness of time had come"). In both cases, God did not ask anyone's permission and did not adjust his schedule to human impatience. The timing itself was part of the miracle — chosen to make clear that what happened could only be explained by God.
Commentary Note — Hagar and Ishmael Expelled (Boice 663–664)
"He requires separations, things you have to turn your back on — friends, occupations, pastimes, whatever it may be — and when you are in the midst of the struggle you look at these things and say, 'What's wrong with them? Aren't they all right?' The difficulty is not that the thing is bad, but that it is not what God called you to." In view of what is coming in Genesis 22, the separation from Ishmael was preparation for the supreme test. God did not ask Abraham to sacrifice Isaac without first asking him to release Ishmael. The lesser separation came first. Each hard thing prepares for a harder thing. The deepest trust is formed in the hardest thing.
The Sacrifice of Isaac
Genesis 22:1–2, 9–12 — The Final Test
"Some time later God tested Abraham. He said to him, 'Abraham!' 'Here I am,' he replied. Then God said, 'Take your son, your only son, whom you love — Isaac — and go to the region of Moriah. Sacrifice him there as a burnt offering on a mountain I will show you.'... When they reached the place God had told him about, Abraham built an altar there and arranged the wood on it. He bound his son Isaac and laid him on the altar, on top of the wood. Then he reached out his hand and took the knife to slay his son. But the angel of the Lord called out to him from heaven, 'Abraham! Abraham!' 'Here I am,' he replied. 'Do not lay a hand on the boy,' he said. 'Do not do anything to him. Now I know that you fear God, because you have not withheld from me your son, your only son.'"
After everything. Decades of waiting, failure, growth. Isaac finally in his arms. And God asked for it all back. Abraham said yes — because a lifetime of experience had taught him that God could be trusted with everything.
Hebrews 11:17–19 — Faith Built on Evidence
"By faith Abraham, when God tested him, offered Isaac as a sacrifice. He who had embraced the promises was about to sacrifice his one and only son, even though God had said to him, 'It is through Isaac that your offspring will be reckoned.' Abraham reasoned that God could even raise the dead, and so in a manner of speaking he did receive Isaac back from death."
This was not blind faith. Abraham reasoned. He had 60–70 years of evidence that God keeps His word. He knew Isaac was the promise. He concluded that if God wanted Isaac back, God could raise him from the dead. That is faith built on a lifetime of walking with God.
Discussion Questions — Take Home
Round 1 — Learning to Walk with God
What have you said you need to take care of before you make time for God?
— or — Have you walked into a bad decision thinking you had it under control? How did that go?
Round 2 — Two Steps Forward, One Step Back
Two steps forward, one step back. Where do you see that pattern in your own walk with God?
Round 3 — Walking Steadier, But Not Perfect
What are some things you think you should be better at or know better by now?
Follow-up: Has God been patient with you in that area — shown up anyway even when you kept stumbling?
