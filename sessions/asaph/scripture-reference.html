"And I heard every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth and in the sea, and all that is in them, saying, 'To him who sits on the throne and to the Lamb be blessing and honor and glory and might forever and ever!'" (5:13)
Revelation 15:3 is the singing: the redeemed sing the song first sung at the Red Sea. Revelation 5:13 is the "every creature" verse, though its verb is "saying." The singers in 5:9 are the elders and living creatures.

Life on the Rotation
The Lots Set the Order, Not the Job
David's 288 trained musicians, "all who were skillful" (1 Chronicles 25:7), were split into 24 divisions of 12. A single drawing of lots set the order of the rotation: "small and great, teacher and pupil alike" (25:8). Each lot went to a division leader, one of the sons of Asaph, Jeduthun, or Heman, and covered "him and his sons and his brothers, twelve" (25:9-31). Every division had the same calling. The lots didn't assign instruments or jobs, and Scripture doesn't name any "favored" slots. What the drawing ignored was rank: status didn't decide your place in the rotation. The same impartial-lot language shows up for the priests (24:31) and gatekeepers (26:13). "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Proverbs 16:33).
Drawn Once, Kept for Generations
Solomon kept "the ordinance of David" for the divisions (2 Chronicles 8:14). At Josiah's Passover, the singers were "in their place according to the command of David, and Asaph, and Heman, and Jeduthun" (35:15). Zechariah, John the Baptist's father, served in "the division of Abijah" (Luke 1:5), the eighth priestly lot from 1 Chronicles 24:10.
What a Turn Looked Like
On duty, the Levites stood "every morning, thanking and praising the LORD, and likewise at evening" (1 Chronicles 23:30), alongside the morning and evening burnt offerings (16:40). Gatekeepers served "every seven days, in turn" (9:25) and lodged overnight (9:27), and shifts changed on the Sabbath (2 Chronicles 23:8). The historian Josephus says a turn lasted a week, Sabbath to Sabbath (Antiquities 7.14.7). Chronicles doesn't state the turn length for the musicians, so the best reading is: most likely a week at a time, about twice a year.
Off Duty
The Levites lived in 48 towns with pastureland (Numbers 35:1-8; Joshua 21), supported by the tithe (Numbers 18:21). After the exile, "the singers had built for themselves villages around Jerusalem" (Nehemiah 12:29). Training passed from father to son (1 Chronicles 25:6-8). Levites more broadly taught the Law (2 Chronicles 17:7-9) and served as officers and judges (1 Chronicles 23:4; 26:29). Whether the musicians did those jobs off duty is an inference.
Chronology: A Tent, Not a Temple
In Asaph's day there was no temple. The ark sat in a tent David pitched in Jerusalem (1 Chronicles 16:1), and Asaph's daily ministry "before the ark" (16:37) happened there. The 24-division system in chapters 23–26 is David organizing ahead for the temple Solomon would build.

The Cutting Room Floor

Research that didn't make the final cut, organized by topic.

The Song of the Sea (Exodus 15)
Genesis 4:21 — Music Shows Up Early
"His brother's name was Jubal; he was the father of all those who play the lyre and pipe."
Music appears just seven generations from Adam, long before anyone sang at the Red Sea. Cut from the live talk to get to the Red Sea faster.
Revelation 15:3 — The Song That Never Ended
"And they sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb, saying, 'Great and amazing are your deeds, O Lord God the Almighty! Just and true are your ways, O King of the nations!'"
In John's vision, those who have conquered stand beside a sea of glass, holding harps of God, and sing "the song of Moses... and the song of the Lamb." The song first sung on the shore of the Red Sea is still being sung at the end of the story. Cut because it's too big a jump in the timeline for the live talk.
The Shape of the Song
The song has two movements. Verses 1-12 look back at what God has just done at the sea. Verses 13-18 look forward to what He will do: leading His people to His holy abode, the nations trembling, and Israel planted on His mountain. Each stanza opens with praise and closes with judgment on the enemy. (Charlie Garrett, The Superior Word, "Exodus 15:1-10: The Song of Moses, the Song at the Sea, Part I.")
Word Studies: Devotional Readings
Garrett's commentary includes word studies and typology: the short divine name Yah (15:2), a link between yeshuah ("salvation," 15:2) and the name Jesus (Yeshua), and etymologies for several other words in the song. These are devotional and typological readings, worth pondering, not established linguistics. His word counts and etymologies should be checked against a Hebrew lexicon before you rely on them. Garrett also calls this the first recorded song in human history. It isn't: older Egyptian and Mesopotamian hymns survive. It is one of the earliest songs recorded in Scripture.
Was It Miriam's Song First?
Some scholars argue the Song of the Sea originally belonged to Miriam and was later attributed to Moses, since her short refrain (15:21) looks like the older core. This is a theory about how the text was composed, not what the text says. As written, Moses and all Israel sing the full song (15:1-18), and Miriam leads the women in the refrain at the same event (15:20-21). Verse 19 is a recap ("For when the horses of Pharaoh..."), so verses 20-21 may describe something happening alongside Moses' song rather than strictly after it. The verb behind "Miriam sang to them" (15:21) is often read as "answered," which suggests a call-and-response between the two groups. That's a common reading, but it's debated.
Exodus 15:1, 21 — "I Will Sing" and "Sing"
"I will sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea." (15:1)
"Sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea." (15:21)
Set side by side, the two lines differ by one change. Moses and Israel open with "I will sing." Miriam's refrain opens with "Sing." Notice too that the whole nation sings "I," not "we": every one of them is making it personal, so worship is personal before it's corporate. Miriam takes that personal song and turns it into a call to everyone. Nobody appointed her to lead. Cut from the live talk to keep the focus on why they sang (God showed up and won), with only a brief mention of Miriam's call to action.
Hannah (1 Samuel 1–2)
1 Samuel 1:3-8 — The Yearly Trip
"Now this man used to go up year by year from his city to worship and to sacrifice to the LORD of hosts at Shiloh." (1:3)
Every year, Elkanah's family made the trip to Shiloh to worship. Every year it was miserable for Hannah: she was childless, and her rival Peninnah provoked her about it. "So it went on year by year" (1:7). She wept and would not eat. Elkanah's attempt at comfort: "Am I not more to you than ten sons?" (1:8). Her worship was an obligation on the calendar that she dreaded.
1 Samuel 1:10-18 — Pouring Out Her Soul
"No, my lord, I am a woman troubled in spirit. I have drunk neither wine nor strong drink, but I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD." (1:15)
She didn't go through the motions of the trip and go home. She prayed so hard, her lips moving with no sound, that Eli the priest decided she was drunk (1:13-14). Even the priest misjudged real worship.
1 Samuel 1:24–2:10 — The Song at the Handoff
"And Hannah prayed and said, 'My heart exults in the LORD; my horn is exalted in the LORD.'" (2:1)
Samuel is born, and Hannah sings on the day she hands him over to serve at Shiloh. Her worship came at the moment she let go. For the guys who say they're not musical: the text says she prayed. No instrument is mentioned. Mary's song (Luke 1:46-55) echoes Hannah's closely. Her song made it into Section 1 as one line in "The Pattern"; the backstory on this page was cut for time. Hannah is the same pattern as the rest of the session: she showed up out of obligation, actually sought God while she was there, and met Him.
Other Songs Before David
Judges 5 — Deborah and Barak
The full song after Sisera's defeat, including a roast of the tribes that stayed home: Reuben deliberating "by the watercourses," Dan staying "with the ships" (5:15-17). Too long and tonally heavy (Jael and the tent peg) for more than a one-line mention.
Judges 11:34 — Jephthah's Daughter
She comes out to meet her father "with tambourines and with dances." It fits the pattern of songs after victory, but the story is far too heavy for a passing reference.
1 Samuel 18:8-9 — The Song That Made Saul Jealous
The women's song after Goliath ("Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands") made Saul angry, "and Saul eyed David from that day on." Its role in Saul's jealousy is off-thread.
1 Samuel 10:5; 16:23 — Prophets with Instruments, David's Lyre
A band of prophets comes down from the high place with harp, tambourine, flute and lyre, prophesying. David plays the lyre for Saul, and the harmful spirit departs. Both bridge to Asaph as "seer," but there wasn't room.
David and Goliath (1 Samuel 17–18)
1 Samuel 17:47; 18:7 — David Knows Who Killed Goliath
"...that all this assembly may know that the LORD saves not with sword and spear. For the battle is the LORD's, and he will give you into our hand." (17:47)
"Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands." (18:7)
After Goliath falls, the women's song credits Saul and David, and God isn't mentioned. But before the fight, David said, "the battle is the LORD's." It's nearly the same scene as Exodus 15: women with tambourines and dancing after a victory. But Miriam sang "Sing to the LORD," and these women sang about the men. Saul's jealousy follows (18:8-9). A victory song isn't automatically worship; what makes it worship is who it's pointed at. Cut from Section 1's "Pattern" (it was originally listed as a song to God, which it isn't) and from Section 2.
Other Victory Songs
Numbers 21:16-18 — The Song of the Well
"Then Israel sang this song: 'Spring up, O well! Sing to it!'" (21:17)
God gives Israel water in the desert, and they sing to the well. Originally in Section 1's "Pattern," replaced by Hannah and Hezekiah.
More Songs After God Acts
2 Samuel 22 / Psalm 18 (David, looking back on a lifetime of rescues); 1 Chronicles 16:7-36 (the song David gives Asaph); 2 Chronicles 20:27-28; Ezra 3:10-13; Nehemiah 12:27-43; Luke 1:46-55 (Mary); Luke 1:68-79 (Zechariah); Revelation 15:3-4.
2 Chronicles 20:14, 21-22; Acts 16:25 — Singing Before the Victory
Jehoshaphat sends singers out ahead of the army, after Jahaziel, "a Levite of the sons of Asaph," prophesies the win (20:14). Paul and Silas sing hymns at midnight in a Philippian jail. Both are worship before the win, so they didn't fit Section 1's pattern.
Psalm 144 (Septuagint heading) — David and Goliath
The Greek Septuagint adds a heading linking Psalm 144 to Goliath. It's a later addition, not in the Hebrew text, so it wasn't used.
Asaph and the Levites
1 Chronicles 23–27; Numbers 18:21-24; Joshua 13:33 — David Organizes the Levites
David organized 38,000 Levites by role, including 4,000 musicians, with the same seriousness as his army. Late in his life, after making Solomon king (23:1), David notes that God has given Israel rest, so the Levites no longer need to carry the tabernacle (23:25-26), and their duties are reassigned, including music: the worship team was made up of former movers. Cut from "Enter Asaph" because it happens years after the ark procession. Levites held no tribal land and lived on the tithe, so leading worship was a fully supported calling.
Who Wrote the Psalms of Asaph?
Twelve psalms carry Asaph's name: Psalm 50 and Psalms 73-83. Some, including 73, fit David's or Solomon's era. Others, including 74 and 79, describe the Babylonian destruction of the temple in 586 BC, centuries after Asaph died, so "of Asaph" often means the guild his sons carried on. A minority theory ties Psalms 74-75 to Shishak's invasion under Rehoboam and Psalms 75 and 82 to disillusionment with Solomon. That chronology is speculative.
Psalm 73: Spurgeon's Notes
A Translation Note on 73:10
Spurgeon's notes on verse 10 depend on the KJV reading, "his people return hither." The ESV reads it very differently: "his people turn back to them." The Hebrew is difficult. Read those notes with the KJV in hand.
Envy Sharpens the Eyesight (73:3)
An old story quoted in Spurgeon's notes on verse 3: doctors were arguing over what sharpens a man's eyesight best, and the winning answer was envy, because it makes everything look bigger. That's exactly Asaph's problem in verses 3-12: the wicked look healthier, richer, and more carefree than they really are. Cut from the live talk because it slowed down the move from the slip to the complaint. (Thomas Le Blanc, quoted in Spurgeon, The Treasury of David.)
Raised High, Fallen Low (73:18)
On "Truly you set them in slippery places," Spurgeon writes: "If the wicked had not been raised so high they could not have fallen so low." The prosperity Asaph envied is the very thing that sets up the fall. Cut from the live talk to keep the focus on the sanctuary moment in verse 17. (Spurgeon, The Treasury of David.)
Pricked in the Kidneys (73:21)
The ESV reads "I was pricked in heart," but the Hebrew says he was pierced in his kidneys, which the KJV renders "reins." In Hebrew thought the kidneys were the seat of a person's deepest emotions, the gut. The closest modern word is "gut-punched." Cut from the live talk to keep the focus on how Asaph sees God after the sanctuary.
"Like a Beast": Behemoth (73:22)
"I was brutish and ignorant; I was like a beast toward you." The Hebrew word for "beast" there is behemoth, the same form God uses for the great creature in Job 40:15. Asaph is saying that in his envy, he was no better than an animal before God. It's a nice link to Job, who appears in the Close, but it was cut to keep the beat short.
Job 38:7 — When the Morning Stars Sang
"...when the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy?"
When God finally answers Job, He takes him back to creation, a moment of worship before anyone existed to sing it. A fitting picture for a session on worship, but cut when Job moved to the Close and the beat was trimmed to the essentials.
Bradwardine's Hermit and the Angel
A medieval story quoted in Spurgeon's notes (also in the Gesta Romanorum and Parnell's poem "The Hermit"). A hermit troubled by the prosperity of the wicked travels with an angel in disguise, who does a series of baffling things, including giving a gold cup to a wicked man and killing an infant because his father loved him too much. At the end the angel explains every reason. Not used live, for two reasons. The infant's death could wound people in a room full of fathers. And the "every reason explained" ending promises more than Psalm 73 does: Asaph gets God's presence, not explanations.
Other Notes Left Out
Spurgeon's "fatted swine" note on verse 7 (it reads as mocking body size at a breakfast table). Calvin on verse 8, "giants, or rather inhuman monsters" (Calvin's context needs checking before use). Haman's gallows as an illustration of verse 18 (it needs too much of the book of Esther to land).
The Warmest Nest (73:4-12)
Spurgeon on the prosperity of the wicked: "He ploughs least, and yet has the most fodder. He deserves the hottest hell, and yet has the warmest nest." (Spurgeon, The Treasury of David, KJV-based.) Cut when the complaint beat was tightened around verses 2-3 and 13.
The Close
2 Chronicles 5:12; 29:13; 35:15; Ezra 2:41; Nehemiah 7:44; 12:35, 46 — The Sons of Asaph
Asaph's descendants led worship for centuries: at the dedication of Solomon's temple, under Hezekiah, at Josiah's Passover, and among the exiles who came home from Babylon. A family that kept showing up for its shift for five hundred years. Cut from the Close for time.
Ezra 3:10-13 — Old Men Weep, Young Men Shout
At the new temple's foundation, the sons of Asaph lead with cymbals and the people "sang responsively" (3:11). The old men who remembered the first temple wept while others shouted for joy, "so that the people could not distinguish the sound of the joyful shout from the sound of the people's weeping" (3:13). Strong legacy material; only verses 10-11 are referenced live.
Psalm 73:23-26 — A Responsive Reading
The planned close was a responsive reading of these verses, leader and room alternating. Replaced by singing the Doxology together, so the session ends with the room actually singing.
Job 1:20-21 — Worship He Didn't Feel
"Then Job arose and tore his robe and shaved his head and fell on the ground and worshiped. And he said, '...The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD.'"
On the day he lost everything, Job's first response was worship. Cut when the Job beat was trimmed for the Close.
Hymn Options Considered
"It Is Well with My Soul" (Horatio Spafford, 1873, written after his four daughters died at sea) was the first plan: worship from inside loss, like Psalm 73. "How Great Thou Art" was ruled out: long verses, a climbing refrain that's hard to sing a cappella, and an English text still under copyright. The Doxology (Thomas Ken, 1674) was chosen because nearly everyone knows it.
Cut Illustrations and Research
The Straws Illustration (Withdrawn)
An early plan had men draw straws on stage to show how the lots worked. After checking 1 Chronicles 25, the lots only set the rotation order, and every division had the same job, so the reveal fell flat. Two other versions were withdrawn as inaccurate: one assigning instruments or jobs by lot, and one using the later priestly daily-task lots (Luke 1:9; Mishnah, Yoma 2 and Tamid), which describe later temple practice, not David's divisions. A possible application line from the same idea: every believer has a place in "a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9). That's application, not something Chronicles says.
Bonus — Singing, Music, and the Brain

Research on what singing and listening to music do for us. It didn't fit naturally into the live talk, but it's worth knowing.

Only About 1 in 70 People Are Truly Tone-Deaf
In a study of 20,000 people, only about 1.5% had congenital amusia, true tone-deafness. That's well below the 4% estimate from an older 1980 test. Most people who say "I can't sing" hear music just fine. (Isabelle Peretz & Dominique T. Vuvan, "Prevalence of congenital amusia," European Journal of Human Genetics, 2017. pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28224991)
"Unmusical" Usually Means "I Don't Like How I Sound"
Interviews with adults who called themselves unmusical found that they judged themselves by how they perform, especially singing, rather than by any problem with hearing music. (John A. Sloboda, Karen J. Wise & Isabelle Peretz, "Quantifying tone deafness in the general population," Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences, 2005. pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16597772)
Singing Together Syncs Your Hearts
In a small study (11 singers analyzed), choir members who sang structured songs in unison breathed together on the phrases, and their heart rates rose and fell together. In the researchers' words, unison singing "makes the hearts of the singers accelerate and decelerate simultaneously." (Björn Vickhoff et al., "Music structure determines heart rate variability of singers," Frontiers in Psychology, 2013. doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00334)
Even in Grief, Singing Lowers Stress
In a preliminary study, cancer patients, their caregivers, and grieving family members had lower stress hormones and better mood after a single hour of choir singing. (Daisy Fancourt et al., ecancermedicalscience, 2016. doi.org/10.3332/ecancer.2016.631)
Singing Does What Listening Doesn't
Singing, drumming, and dancing all raised people's pain tolerance, a stand-in for the brain's own endorphins. Thirty minutes of just listening to music did not. One test compared a church service full of singing with a prayer meeting that had no music. (Robin Dunbar et al., "Performance of music elevates pain threshold and positive affect," Evolutionary Psychology, 2012. pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23089077)
Sing It or Just Hear It?
The same 31 choir members were tested twice: once after singing at rehearsal, once after listening to recorded choral music. After singing, their mood improved and they had more germ-fighting antibodies in their saliva. After listening, their mood got worse. Maybe they would rather have been singing. The antibody is secretory immunoglobulin A (S-IgA). It coats the mouth, nose, and throat and is one of the body's first defenses against colds. This was one measurement right after one rehearsal, so it shows a short-term boost, not that choir members get fewer colds. (Gunter Kreutz et al., "Effects of choir singing or listening on secretory immunoglobulin A, cortisol, and emotional state," Journal of Behavioral Medicine, 2004. pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15669447)
Your Brain Has Neurons Just for Singing
Recordings from electrodes in patients' brains found a group of neurons in the auditory cortex that responds to singing, and hardly at all to speech or instrumental music. (Sam Norman-Haignere et al., "A neural population selective for song in human auditory cortex," Current Biology, 2022. doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.01.069)
Music You Love Releases Dopamine
Brain scans caught dopamine, the brain's reward chemical, being released in two places: one while people anticipated the best part of a song, another at the moment they got chills. Music isn't food or money, yet the brain treats it as a reward. A follow-up showed dopamine is the cause: a drug that raised it made music more enjoyable, and one that blocked it took the chills away. (Valorie Salimpoor et al., Nature Neuroscience, 2011, doi.org/10.1038/nn.2726; Laura Ferreri et al., PNAS, 2019, pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30670642)
Old Songs Bring Back Memories
One area at the front of the brain, the medial prefrontal cortex, links familiar songs with personal memories and emotions. The stronger the memory a song brought back, the more that area lit up. It's why the hymns you grew up singing can take you right back. (Petr Janata, "The neural architecture of music-evoked autobiographical memories," Cerebral Cortex, 2009. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2758676)
When Speech Is Gone, the Song Can Remain
People who lose speech to damage on the left side of the brain can often sing phrases they can't say. Therapists build on this with Melodic Intonation Therapy, which appears to work through the right side of the brain. (Gottfried Schlaug, Sarah Marchina & Andrea Norton, "From singing to speaking," Music Perception, 2008. musicianbrain.com)
Singing Together Bonds a Group
Singing breaks the ice: groups that sang together bonded faster than groups doing other activities (Eiluned Pearce, Jacques Launay & Robin Dunbar, Royal Society Open Science, 2015, doi.org/10.1098/rsos.150221), and the effect held in a very large choir (Daniel Weinstein et al., Evolution and Human Behavior, 2016, doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2015.10.002). Musical memory is preserved in Alzheimer's disease (Jörn-Henrik Jacobsen et al., Brain, 2015, doi.org/10.1093/brain/awv135). Ephesians 5:19 and Colossians 3:16 ("addressing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs") would have bridged to the bonding research.
