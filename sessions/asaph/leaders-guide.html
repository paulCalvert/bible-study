Leader's Guide

Asaph: Worshipping God, Even When It's Hard

Event: CMBC Men's Breakfast  |  Date: November 7, 2026  |  Materials by: Paul Calvert  |  Scripture: ESV unless noted
Core Thread

Worship isn't a musical-aptitude test or a performance of having it all together. It's honest engagement with God, especially on the days we don't feel it.

The arc of the morning: worship that only reacts to God's victories (Section 1), worship that is appointed and shows up on schedule (Section 2), and what happens when the man whose job is praise can't find it (Section 3). The pattern to watch for: show up out of obligation → actually seek Him → meet Him → wonder.

Intro — Before Prayer  (~6 min)
1. Christian Worship: A Lot of Options and Opinions (~30 sec)
  • Musical worship is a more divisive topic than we like to admit. Hymns or modern songs. Organ, band, or no instruments at all. Hands raised or hands in pockets. Everyone has an opinion about what "real" worship looks like.
2. Cedar Mill Worship, ~20 Years Ago (~1 min)
  • Years ago, Cedar Mill's worship looked a little different. It was a lot more reserved. Pastor Carl once told the congregation, half-joking:
    "If you feel so moved by the Spirit, you are allowed to raise your hands." — Pastor Carl
3. "Not My Thing" (~1.5 min)
  • Here's where I land. Musical worship has always been hard for me to get into, for a few reasons:
    • I don't want to fake it, so I won't do it if I'm not feeling it.
    • I worship in "other" ways.
    • It's not my song, or not my style.
  • And some of that is valid. Worship isn't only music. For some of us it looks like serving, studying, or time alone with God.
  • But it doesn't excuse us from singing.
4. Some Interesting Studies (~1.5 min)
  • So I did some digging into the research on singing. Singing might be worth revisiting.
  • Only about 1 in 70 people are truly tone-deaf. Most guys who say "I can't sing" hear music just fine. They just don't like how they sound. In one study of about 20,000 people, roughly 1.5% had true tone-deafness. Adults who call themselves "unmusical" tend to judge themselves by performance, not by any problem with their hearing.
  • Singing together syncs your hearts. One small study found that when a choir sings together, they breathe together on the phrases, and their heart rates rise and fall together.
  • Even in grief, singing lowers stress. In a preliminary study, cancer patients and grieving caregivers had lower stress and better mood after a single choir session.
  • In Revelation, we're all singing. The redeemed sing "the song of Moses" (Revelation 15:3), and every creature in heaven and on earth joins the praise (Revelation 5:13). Might as well start practicing now!
  • (Research links and fuller summaries are in the Scripture Reference.)
5. Who Was Leading the Band? (~1 min)
  • Last month we watched David dance before the ark with everything he had. Michal watched from a window, and we spent some time on her side of that story.
  • But here's a question nobody asked last month: who was leading the band?
  • His name was Asaph.
6. Pray (~30 sec)
  • Pray, then move into Section 1.
Section 1 — When the Song Just Came Out  (~10.5 min | Exodus 14–15, Judges 5, 1 Samuel 2, Isaiah 38, 1 Samuel 27–30)
"Before anyone had Asaph's job, worship was a reaction. Something happened, and the song came out."
1. Before There Was a Worship Team (~30 sec)
  • For centuries there was no choir, no rotation, no schedule. People sang when something had just happened.
2. The Red Sea (~4 min)
Exodus 14:10, 21-22; 15:1-2, 20-21
  • Israel, slaves in Egypt. Moses shows up. Ten plagues. Badaboom. Israel is finally free from Egypt, but Pharaoh changes his mind and sends his army after them. They're pinned against the sea, and they cry out to the LORD (Exodus 14:10).
  • And God saves them, in about the most dramatic way possible. He parts the Red Sea, and they walk through on dry ground with a wall of water on either side (14:21-22). They go in terrified. They come out the other side singing.
  • Moses and all Israel sing (Exodus 15:1-18). And listen to what they sing about. Not how they feel. What God just did:
    "I will sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea. The LORD is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation." — Exodus 15:1-2
  • And men are singing here. Not a choir, not the musical guys. Everybody.
  • Moses sings, "I will sing to the LORD." Then Miriam picks up a tambourine, the women follow her, dancing, and she turns it into a call to action: "Sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously" (15:20-21).
  • God showed up, and God won. The song is the response.
3. The Pattern (~1.5 min)
Judges 5:1-3; 1 Samuel 2:1-10; Isaiah 38:9-20
  • And it's not just the Red Sea. Over and over, God shows up and wins, and praise follows:
    • God routs Sisera's army, and Deborah and Barak sing: "to the LORD I will sing; I will make melody to the LORD, the God of Israel" (Judges 5:1-3).
    • Hannah prays for years for a son. God gives her Samuel, and she sings: "My heart exults in the LORD" (1 Samuel 2:1-10).
    • King Hezekiah is sick to the point of death. God heals him, and he writes: "The LORD will save me, and we will play my music on stringed instruments all the days of our lives, at the house of the LORD" (Isaiah 38:9-20).
  • The sea parts, the enemy falls, the prayer is answered, the sick man is healed, and then they sing. Every song comes after a win.
4. What's Wrong With This Model? (~1 min)
  • So what's wrong with this kind of worship? Nothing. It's real, and it's right. God acted, and His people praised Him.
  • It's just not sustainable. What happens when God goes quiet?
5. Don't Be Fooled: Three Days Later (~2 min)
Exodus 15:22-24
  • Go back to that crowd on the shore of the Red Sea. Three days into the wilderness, they find water, and it's bitter. "And the people grumbled against Moses, saying, 'What shall we drink?'" (Exodus 15:24)
  • Spontaneous worship is real. It's also got about a three-day shelf life.
6. Landing: King David's Take on This (~1.5 min)
1 Samuel 27–30:6
  • Worship that runs on how we feel only shows up when we're winning.
  • David, the master psalmist, knows this problem firsthand. He knows what it's like not to feel like worshipping God. In despair, he fled from Saul and served in Gath for a period of time (1 Samuel 27–30:6).
  • And David knows we need to worship God every day, no matter how we feel. So he put it on the schedule.

Section 2 — Structured → Natural  (~17 min | 1 Samuel 5–7, 2 Samuel 5–6, Numbers 4, 1 Chronicles 6, 13–16, 23, 25, Proverbs 16)
"David's answer to the three-day shelf life was appointed, scheduled, carefully done worship. And structure turned out not to be the enemy of heart."
1. David Wants the Ark Home (~1 min)
2 Samuel 5:6-7; 6:1-2; 1 Samuel 7:1-2; 1 Chronicles 13:3
  • David is king now. He's taken Jerusalem and made it his city (2 Samuel 5:6-7).
  • But the ark of the covenant, the place where God's presence rested among His people, isn't there. It's been sitting in a private house in Kiriath-jearim for at least twenty years (1 Samuel 7:1-2). "We did not seek it in the days of Saul" (1 Chronicles 13:3).
  • David wants it in Jerusalem. But there's a problem. What is it?
2. The Ark in Philistia (~1.5 min)
1 Samuel 5:1-11
  • A toppled idol. The Philistines capture the ark in battle and set it in the temple of their god, Dagon. The next morning, Dagon is lying face down in front of the ark. They stand him back up. The next morning he's face down again, with his head and both hands broken off on the threshold (1 Samuel 5:1-4).
  • A plague. It follows the ark from city to city. The LORD strikes Ashdod with tumors (5:6). They move the ark to Gath: tumors there too (5:9). They move it to Ekron, and the people scream, "They have brought around to us the ark of the God of Israel to kill us and our people" (5:10). "There was a deathly panic throughout the whole city" (5:11).
  • So they send it back to Israel on a cart.
3. Beth-shemesh (~1 min)
1 Samuel 6:19-20
  • The cart arrives in Beth-shemesh. The men of the city look into the ark, and God strikes down seventy of them (6:19). Did they not watch Raiders of the Lost Ark?
  • "Who is able to stand before the LORD, this holy God?" (1 Samuel 6:20)
4. First Jerusalem Journey: Did NOT Go Well (~1.5 min)
2 Samuel 6:3-12
  • David's first attempt. They put the ark on a new cart, the same way the Philistines sent it back (2 Samuel 6:3). The oxen stumble, Uzzah reaches out to steady the ark, and God strikes him dead on the spot (6:6-7).
  • After that, David leaves the ark at the house of a man named Obed-edom, where it stays for three months before he tries again (6:10-12).
  • That's the problem. The ark is the presence of a holy God, and you don't handle Him however you like.
5. Doing It Right This Time (~1 min)
1 Chronicles 15:2, 12-17; Numbers 4:15
  • Second attempt, and no cart rides. David owns the failure: the first time went wrong "because we did not seek him according to the rule" (1 Chronicles 15:13). This time, "No one but the Levites may carry the ark of God" (15:2).
  • And notice: the Levites haven't done this in a long time. The ark sat in one house for at least twenty years and through all of Saul's reign. Nobody alive had carried it. They're going back to instructions written down in Moses' day (Numbers 4:15).
  • The Levites consecrate themselves and carry it on poles on their shoulders, "as Moses had commanded" (1 Chronicles 15:14-15). No cart this time.
  • And David tells the chiefs of the Levites to appoint singers and musicians for the procession. One of the men they appoint, alongside Heman and Ethan, is named Asaph (1 Chronicles 15:16-17).
  • Nothing about this is casual.
6. Enter Asaph (~2 min)
1 Chronicles 6:39-43; Numbers 4:24-26; 1 Chronicles 15:19; 16:5, 7
  • Asaph is a Levite, the son of Berechiah, from the clan of Gershon. His family line runs all the way back to Gershom, son of Levi (1 Chronicles 6:39-43).
  • His family's chore: in the wilderness, the Gershonites carried the tabernacle's curtains, coverings, screens and cords (Numbers 4:24-26). They were the movers.
  • His instrument: bronze cymbals (15:19). Before long he's the chief musician before the ark (16:5), and the first man David hands a song of thanks to (16:7).
  • Israel's first appointed worship leader came from a family that hauled tent curtains.
7. Everyone at Ground Zero (~2 min)
1 Chronicles 15:25-27; 2 Samuel 6:9
  • Picture the scene before the first step. The elders and the commanders. The Levites with the poles on their shoulders. The singers in fine linen robes, Chenaniah leading the music, Asaph with his cymbals, and David himself in a linen robe (1 Chronicles 15:25-27).
  • Nobody here is "feeling it." They had all heard the stories of Uzzah and Beth-shemesh. And David? Remember what he said the last time he tried this:
    "And David was afraid of the LORD that day, and he said, 'How can the ark of the LORD come to me?'" — 2 Samuel 6:9
    Three months later, he's standing at the front of the procession, scared of getting it wrong again.
  • This is worship as obligation. They're not focused on feeling anything. They're focused on getting it right.
8. Six Steps. Sacrifice. Repeat. (~1.5 min)
2 Samuel 6:13; 1 Chronicles 15:26
  • "And when those who bore the ark of the LORD had gone six steps, he sacrificed an ox and a fattened animal." — 2 Samuel 6:13
  • Six steps, and nobody died. Sacrifice. Six more steps. Sacrifice. Lather, rinse, repeat. (Interpretive: the verse can be read as one sacrifice after the first six steps, or a sacrifice every six steps. Readers differ. Present it as one reading.)
  • And then this: "because God helped the Levites who were carrying the ark," they sacrificed seven bulls and seven rams (1 Chronicles 15:26).
  • They showed up out of obligation, they did it carefully, and God met them on the way.
9. Structured Worship Becomes Natural Worship (~1 min)
2 Samuel 6:14-15; 1 Chronicles 15:28
  • Somewhere along the way, they stopped counting steps, and they began dancing. "David danced before the LORD with all his might" (2 Samuel 6:14), with all Israel shouting, horns, trumpets, cymbals, harps and lyres (1 Chronicles 15:28).
  • It started with counting steps. It ended with leaps of joy.
10. A Daily Shift (~3 min)
2 Samuel 6:20-23; 1 Chronicles 16:1, 4, 37; 23:1, 30; 25:7-8; Proverbs 16:33
  • All in all, Asaph had a good day! We know David has a marital spat when he gets home (2 Samuel 6:20-23), but as for Asaph, he's still alive! And it was a rockin' worship service!
  • On top of that, he got a permanent promotion. Once the ark is in place, in a tent David pitched for it in Jerusalem (1 Chronicles 16:1), Asaph gets his job description: "to invoke, to thank, and to praise the LORD" (16:4). "Invoke" means calling on God. Lament already has a place on the job description. And David leaves Asaph before the ark "to minister regularly before the ark as each day required" (16:37). Each day. Not each victory.
  • Years later, near the end of his life, David organized the musicians for the temple Solomon would build (1 Chronicles 23:1). He split them into 24 divisions and cast lots once to set the rotation. The text says they drew "small and great, teacher and pupil alike" (1 Chronicles 25:8). Skill, seniority, and authority played no part in the order. The newest apprentice's division could serve before his teacher's. And Proverbs says, "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Proverbs 16:33). They left the order to God. (Precision: skill played no part in the order. All 288 musicians were "trained in singing to the LORD, all who were skillful," 25:7.)
  • (Optional.) What a turn looked like: when your week came, you stood "every morning, thanking and praising the LORD, and likewise at evening" (23:30), and you likely stayed close by the whole week. Most likely a week at a time, Sabbath to Sabbath, about twice a year. The rest of the time, you went home to your town, lived on Israel's tithes, and trained the next generation.
  • Status didn't decide your place in the rotation. Worship isn't reserved for the experts.
11. Landing: Keep Showing Up (~1.5 min)
  • David put worship on a schedule, for the bad days and the boring ones, when there's no victory to celebrate.
  • You don't show up because you feel like it. You show up because it's your week. And you've got five more days to go.
  • Worship can start as an obligation. Keep showing up, and somewhere along the way, it becomes real.

Section 3 — Until...  (~15 min | Psalm 73)
"When we need to worship but don't want to. Asaph, whose job was praise, nearly lost his faith. His post, the sanctuary, is where it turned."
Intro (~30 sec)
Psalms 50, 73–83
  • So here's the question. What happens when the guy whose whole job is praise shows up for his shift and can't do it?
  • Twelve psalms carry Asaph's name (Psalms 50, 73–83). He wrote some of them himself. Others came from the guild of musicians who carried on his name for generations.
1. Psalm 73: Asaph's Revelation (~30 sec)
Psalms 73; 74; 79
  • Psalm 73 was likely written by Asaph himself. Nothing in it historically contradicts that (like the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem described in Psalms 74 and 79), and it reads very much as a personal song.
2. A Strong Opening... (~30 sec)
Psalm 73:1
  • It opens with the conclusion: "Truly God is good to Israel, to those who are pure in heart" (73:1). He's telling a story whose ending he already knows.
  • "God is good." Just what you want your worship pastor to say. But it goes on...
3. The Big BUT (~2 min)
Psalm 73:2-13
  • And then the big "but." And I cannot lie:
    "But as for me, my feet had almost stumbled, my steps had nearly slipped. For I was envious of the arrogant when I saw the prosperity of the wicked." — Psalm 73:2-3
  • Not pure in heart. Why? (Rhetorical, for the room. He just said God is good to the pure in heart, and then, "But as for me..." The implication is that he's not. And he answers it right away: envy.)
  • Verses 4–12, summarized: the wicked are healthy, rich, proud, carefree, and nobody stops them.
  • And then the low point: "All in vain have I kept my heart clean and washed my hands in innocence" (73:13).
4. Asaph the Seer: Praising AND Proclaiming God (~1.5 min)
2 Chronicles 29:30; 1 Chronicles 25:1-2; Exodus 15:20
  • Remember who's writing this. Asaph isn't just the band leader. Scripture calls him a "seer" (2 Chronicles 29:30), and says he and his sons "prophesied" with lyres, harps and cymbals (1 Chronicles 25:1-2). Miriam, leading the women's song at the Red Sea, is called a prophetess too (Exodus 15:20). Along with leading worship, the job was proclaiming God to the people.
  • Don't be fooled. We assume the worship guys have it figured out.
    "Asaph was a seer, but he could not see when reason left him in the dark." — Spurgeon, The Treasury of David
  • This is the worship guy, the seer, and he couldn't see it.
5. He Didn't Want to Bring Everyone Down (~1.5 min)
Psalm 73:15-16
  • "If I had said, 'I will speak thus,' I would have betrayed the generation of your children" (73:15). He's the man leading the songs. If he says this out loud, he tells everyone their faith is pointless.
  • So he kept it to himself. And it wore him out: "it seemed to me a wearisome task" (73:16).
  • He didn't want to bring everyone down, so he was carrying it alone.
6. UNTIL (~2 min)
Psalm 73:16-17
  • Stop on one word: "until." It's a small word, but it means something is about to change. Something happens, and things turn out different than you thought. Everything Asaph has been building up for sixteen verses is about to be turned on its head.
  • "...until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end." — Psalm 73:17
  • Remember Marah? Israel took its complaint to Moses. Asaph took his straight into the sanctuary, to the Lord. Same frustration, different destination.
  • The sanctuary was his post. The place David assigned him. Each day, as each day required. We don't know how long he wrestled with this. It could have been a day, a week, or much longer. But he kept coming back to worship, and he didn't just stand there.
  • Spurgeon: "He shifted his point of view, and apparent disorder resolved itself into harmony." A musical picture, right at the turning point.
  • He kept showing up, and he went in, looking.
7. Blessing? Or a Curse? (~1.5 min)
Psalm 73:17-19, 23
  • "...then I discerned their end. Truly you set them in slippery places." — Psalm 73:17-18
  • The prosperity Asaph envied turns out to be the trap. God set them on that smooth ground, and it's how they fall to ruin, "destroyed in a moment" (73:18-19). And God doesn't leave the ones He loves standing on it alone. Asaph's own feet "had nearly slipped" (73:2), but a few verses later, God is holding his right hand (73:23).
  • What looked like a blessing is really a curse for them.
8. Nevertheless (~2 min)
Psalm 73:21-26
  • Now that he can see again, Asaph looks back at himself honestly. Bitter, gut-punched, "brutish and ignorant... like a beast toward you" (73:21-22). Not a flattering picture.
  • And then he re-evaluates everything:
    "Nevertheless, I am continually with you; you hold my right hand. You guide me with your counsel, and afterward you will receive me to glory. Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you. My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever." — Psalm 73:23-26
  • God never let go of him, even while he was envying, complaining and acting like an animal. Spurgeon notes these mercies were "given to one who confessed himself a fool," so "we also may hope to enjoy them." He walked in envying what they had. Now look at what he wants.
  • They have the prosperity. He has God. And he wouldn't trade.
9. What Started Out Hidden, Brought Into the Light (~1.5 min)
Psalm 73:15, 28
  • Remember why he kept it to himself: he didn't want to betray "the generation of your children" (73:15). He wasn't hiding from everyone. He was protecting the young in the faith from a struggle they weren't ready to carry.
  • And yet he ended up publishing a psalm about it, for the whole congregation to sing, once he'd taken it to God.
  • That's not "figure it all out before you're allowed to have doubts." It's this: don't air it out for everyone. Find men you trust, your peers or men further along in the faith, and talk it through with them. Don't put the weight of it on those younger than you. And take it to God.
  • Don't carry it alone. Bring it into the light, with the right people, and before God.
10. Landing: Good to Be Near God (~1.5 min)
Psalm 73:28
  • "But for me it is good to be near God; I have made the Lord GOD my refuge, that I may tell of all your works." — Psalm 73:28
  • Showing up only matters if you show up looking for Him.
  • Spontaneous worship happens when you feel it. Organized worship carries you when you don't.

Close  (~3.5 min)
1. When the Sanctuary Is Quiet: Job (~2 min)
Job 23:3, 8-10; 42:5
  • Some of you are thinking, "I show up. I look for Him. And I still can't find Him." Not every shift ends with a sanctuary moment.
  • Job knew that feeling, chapter after chapter:
    "Oh, that I knew where I might find him... Behold, I go forward, but he is not there." — Job 23:3, 8
    He kept going anyway: "But he knows the way that I take; when he has tried me, I shall come out as gold" (23:10).
  • The encounter did come. It just took until chapter 38. "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you" (Job 42:5).
  • Asaph's turn came when he walked into the sanctuary. Job's took thirty-seven chapters. Both of them kept showing up.
  • This doesn't hinge on how good you are at worship or how hard you try. And it isn't phoning it in. It's showing up on the days you don't want to, looking for God.
  • You may not see Him show up the way Job did, and that's not on you. He isn't going to show up because you're trying really hard, or because you sound wonderful.
  • But on the days He does show up, you want to be there too.
2. Close With the Doxology (~1.5 min)
  • Let's close by singing together, the way the sons of Asaph led Israel in praise. No band. Worship was never about the instrument.
  • Sing a cappella (starting pitch around F). This replaces the closing prayer.

    Praise God, from whom all blessings flow;

    Praise Him, all creatures here below;

    Praise Him above, ye heav'nly host;

    Praise Father, Son, and Holy Ghost.

    Amen.
