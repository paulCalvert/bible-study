Melchizedek
King of Salem. Priest of God Most High. He appears in three verses in Genesis, once in Psalm 110, and becomes a sustained theological argument in Hebrews 5–7. Notes on his historical significance, his function as a type of Christ, and the meaning of a priest who is also a king. Sources: Boice, F.B. Meyer, Arthur Pink.

Historical Context — Genesis 14

King of Righteousness — his character was the point, not just his title Boice 501
Melchizedek as "king of righteousness" (the meaning of his name) and "king of Salem" (king of peace) is not just wordplay. Boice emphasizes that his upright life was a significant contrast to the kings of the plain in that era — men who had just been at war and whose cities would later be destroyed for their wickedness. Melchizedek stood apart morally, not just theologically.

Typology — Priest and King

F.B. Meyer — how the two roles blended throughout Jesus' earthly life F.B. Meyer
Meyer offers an extended reflection on what it means that Jesus is simultaneously priest and king — and how the two roles wove together without contradiction throughout his life and ministry:

Pitying and helping the suffering → priest.
Commanding wind and wave → king.
Entering Jerusalem on a donkey → king.
Interceding for his disciples in John 17 → priest.
Bearing the cross → priest offering sacrifice.
Ascending to the throne at the right hand of the Father → king.
Making ongoing intercession for believers (Heb 7:25) → priest.

The two offices are not sequential — they operate simultaneously and in constant tension, which is exactly what makes Melchizedek the right type. Melchizedek held both offices at once, in a way that no Levitical priest could.

Hebrews — The Theological Argument

Levi paid tithes through Abraham — the lesser is blessed by the greater Boice 513 · Heb 7:4–10
Hebrews 7:4–10 makes a remarkable argument: when Abraham paid tithes to Melchizedek, Levi (Abraham's great-grandson, the ancestor of all Levitical priests) was "in the loins of Abraham" and therefore paid tithes too. This means the entire Levitical priesthood — the one that would later bless Israel, receive tithes, and mediate the covenant — was subordinate to Melchizedek. The lesser is blessed by the greater. Melchizedek outranks the Levitical order. So does Jesus, who is a priest in Melchizedek's order.

Only the high priest entered the Most Holy Place — once a year, only after sacrifice Boice 515
Under the Levitical system, access to God was severely restricted. The Most Holy Place could only be entered by the high priest, only once per year (on Yom Kippur), and only after offering the required sacrifice for sin. Greek and Roman temples by contrast were open to worshippers. The Jewish restriction was not a design flaw — it was a theological statement about sin, holiness, and the distance between man and God. This restriction sets up the full weight of what Melchizedek represents: a priest who offers access that the Levitical system can only gesture toward.

Melchizedek is declared "living" — contrast with priests who die Boice 513 · Heb 7:8
Hebrews 7:8 draws a contrast between the Levitical priests (mortal men who received tithes and then died) and Melchizedek, of whom "it is testified that he lives." This is not a claim about Melchizedek's personal immortality — it is a typological statement. Because his death is not recorded in Scripture, he functions as a type of an eternal priesthood. Jesus fulfills this type perfectly: he is "a priest forever, in the order of Melchizedek" (Ps 110:4), and his priesthood does not pass to a successor because he does not die.

Arthur Pink — On Careful Consideration

11 Greek words for "consider" — each with a distinct shade of meaning Arthur Pink · Heb 10:24–25
Pink notes that the Greek New Testament uses at least 11 different words translated "consider" — each carrying a different nuance of the act:

Serious observing.
Careful deliberation.
Investigating as a watchman scans for threats.
Turning something over in the mind repeatedly.
Examining as one examines evidence.

The range matters because God does not call us to a passive or vague attention to truth. Hebrews 10:24–25 ("consider how to stir up one another to love and good works") uses a word implying active, focused, goal-directed contemplation. Pink's point: the spiritual life requires deliberate mental engagement — not just passive exposure to truth but active consideration of it.
