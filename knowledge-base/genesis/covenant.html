The Covenant Ceremony
Genesis 15:7–21. God ratifies his promise to Abram through an ancient covenant-cutting ceremony — and passes through the pieces alone, making the oath entirely his own. Notes on the eternality of the promise ("I have given"), the distinction between Abram's question and Zechariah's doubt, and the cultural background of the ceremony itself.

Notes

God uses the present tense — "I have given" — indicating the promise is already done Boice 564
In Genesis 15:7, God says "I am the LORD who brought you out of Ur of the Chaldeans to give you this land to possess it" — and in the covenant ratification of verses 18–21, the language shifts to present tense: not "I will give" but "I have given your descendants this land." Boice notes this as an indication of the eternalness and certainty of God's promises to Abram. From God's vantage point, the promise is not pending — it is already accomplished. The land has already been given; the descendants will simply walk into what is already theirs. This is the same logic behind Romans 8:30 ("those he justified, he also glorified") — God speaks of future certainties in the past tense because from his perspective they are settled.

"How can I know?" was not unbelief — it was a request for confirmation Boice 561
On the surface, Abram's question in Genesis 15:8 sounds like the same question Zechariah asks in Luke 1:18 — "How can I know this?" Zechariah was struck mute as a consequence. Abram was not. The difference is in the posture behind the question. Zechariah's question came from skepticism about whether God could do what he had promised. Abram's question came from a desire for deeper understanding and a firmer grasp on what God had already confirmed. It is the same question Gideon asks with the fleece — not defiance, but a hunger for certainty that honors how large the promise is. God's response to Abram is not a rebuke. It is a covenant ceremony.

Ancient covenant ceremony — animals split, parties walked between the halves Boice 562
In the ancient near east, formal covenants were sealed through a ritual that gave them their name — "cutting a covenant." Animals were divided along the backbone and placed in two rows, creating a path between the pieces. The parties making the covenant would walk between the halves and speak their promises there. The meaning was explicit: "May what happened to these animals happen to me if I violate what I have sworn here." The oath was sacred because it was sealed with shed blood, and its violation was not just a broken contract — it was a profound dishonor and an invitation to one's own death.

In Genesis 15, God alone passes through the pieces (as a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch — Gen 15:17). Abram does not walk. This is the extraordinary thing: the covenant is unilateral. God bound himself and asked nothing of Abram in return at that moment. The entire weight of the oath fell on God.

The firepot, the torch, and God walking alone — the promise rests entirely on him Boice 563
God appears in the ceremony as two symbols: a smoking firepot and a flaming torch. Each carries its own theological weight.

The smoking firepot — used to purify metals by burning away impurities. Ties to 1 Peter 1:7 ("the tested genuineness of your faith, more precious than gold that perishes though it is tested by fire") and Malachi 3:3 ("he will sit as a refiner and purifier of silver"). God's presence in the fire is not destructive — it is purifying. The same fire that could consume is here the presence that refines.

The flaming torch — light in darkness, a symbol of God as the source of light. Ties to John 1:5 ("The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it"). God himself is the illumination moving through the night of this ceremony.

God alone walks between the pieces. Abram is in a deep sleep (Gen 15:12). He does not walk. In a standard covenant ceremony both parties passed through together, each binding themselves to the oath. Here, only God moves. This means the promise is not contingent on Abram — it is not a bilateral contract that Abram can break by failing his end. It is God's unilateral oath, staked entirely on his own faithfulness. If this covenant fails, it is God himself who has failed. He took that weight alone.
