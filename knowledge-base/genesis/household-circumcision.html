Circumcision of the Household
Genesis 17:23–27. Abraham circumcises his entire household — including Ishmael and every male servant — that same day. Notes on the significance of including children in the covenant sign, what it means that Ishmael was included, and what the NT's silence on infant baptism may indicate.

Notes

Children included in the covenant sign — parallel to the baptism question Boice 590
The covenant sign in Genesis 17 was given not just to Abraham but to every male in his household — including infants born into it. Boice notes the significance of this inclusion: children were brought under the covenant sign before they could understand or choose it. This becomes a foundational data point in the debate over infant baptism. If circumcision functioned as the covenant sign for the household of faith in the OT — including children — the question for the NT is whether baptism, as the fulfillment of that sign, carries the same household logic (Col 2:11–12).

Ishmael circumcised — included in the covenant sign Boice 592
Ishmael was 13 years old when circumcised. Though Isaac would be the child of promise, Ishmael was not excluded from the covenant sign — Abraham included him along with the rest of the household. Boice notes this as significant: the sign was given broadly, not only to those who were the direct line of promise. God had also made promises to Ishmael specifically (Gen 17:20 — "I will make him fruitful and will greatly increase his numbers"). The covenant sign does not automatically guarantee salvation, but it marks inclusion in the community of covenant people.

NT silence on infant baptism suggests broad agreement at the time Boice 593
The NT contains no controversy about whether infants should be baptized — a notable absence given how many other practices the early church debated vigorously (circumcision, food sacrificed to idols, tongues, roles of women, etc.). Boice argues this silence is itself evidence: if infant baptism had been a departure from prior practice, it would have generated debate. The lack of controversy suggests the early church treated it as a natural extension of the OT household covenant pattern — children included in the sign of the community of faith — rather than a new and disputed innovation.
