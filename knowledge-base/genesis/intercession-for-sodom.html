Abraham Intercedes for Sodom
Genesis 18:16–33. The visitors leave for Sodom. God pauses and tells Abraham what is about to happen. Abraham stands before the LORD and begins to negotiate — fifty righteous, forty-five, forty, thirty, twenty, ten. Notes on what God hears that we have grown numb to, and how Abraham's intercession worked.

Notes

God is not ignorant of any wickedness — not indifferent to any cry of the oppressed Boice 613
"God is not ignorant of any wickedness. He is not indifferent to any cry of the oppressed."

The language of Genesis 18:20–21 — "the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great and their sin is very grave" — is not God learning something new. It is God making explicit, for Abraham's sake, what he already knows and has already heard. The cries of the oppressed reach God. He is not unaware and he is not unmoved. The passage is an assurance that nothing hidden from men is hidden from him, and nothing that breaks the heart of the vulnerable escapes his attention.

Why can't we hear the same cries God hears? — we have grown numb Boice 614
Boice asks the question directly: if God hears the cries of the oppressed and is moved to act, why don't we? He lists out the categories of wickedness and suffering that God hears — the cries of the poor, the abused, the trafficked, the abandoned, the voiceless — that we have grown so accustomed to that we no longer register them as urgent. We have learned to live alongside suffering without being disturbed by it. God has not. The contrast between God's sensitivity to injustice and our own numbness is itself a call to repentance and reorientation — to ask God to let us hear what he hears.

Abraham could intercede because he appealed to God's own nature Boice 615
Abraham's intercession works because he does not ask God to make an exception — he asks God to be who God is. "Far be it from you to do such a thing — to kill the righteous with the wicked... Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Gen 18:25). Abraham is not negotiating against God; he is appealing to God's own character. He grounds every petition in what God has revealed about himself — his justice, his mercy, his distinction between the righteous and the wicked. This is the model for intercession: not trying to change God's mind, but holding God to his own promises and nature. It is the same logic Paul uses in Romans 8:31–34.
